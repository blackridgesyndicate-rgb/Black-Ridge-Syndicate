# Mock Exam 1 — Answer Key with Explanations


## Section 1: Safety & Employee Protection

**1. Answer: C** — *Fall protection — low-slope roof, narrow building*

For roofing work on low-slope roofs, 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10) allows a safety monitoring system alone when the roof is 50 ft or less in width. This roof is 42 ft wide, so a monitor alone is permitted.

- **A** is wrong: A warning line only marks an area. It must be combined with guardrails, nets, PFAS, or a safety monitor, because work still happens between the line and the edge.
- **B** is wrong: Toeboards protect people below from falling objects. They do not stop a worker from falling.
- **D** is wrong: "Stay back when possible" is not a fall-protection system recognized by OSHA.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10); 1926.502(h)

**2. Answer: A** — *Fall protection — steep residential roof*

A 7:12 roof is a steep roof (greater than 4:12). Steep roofs require guardrails with toeboards, safety nets, or PFAS (1926.501(b)(11)). For residential construction (1926.501(b)(13)), conventional protection is presumed feasible. PFAS on properly installed anchors is the standard, compliant choice.

- **B** is wrong: Warning lines and safety monitors are only permitted for roofing work on low-slope roofs (4:12 or less).
- **C** is wrong: A monitor must be on the same walking/working surface as the workers, and monitors aren't permitted on steep roofs anyway.
- **D** is wrong: Slide guards alone are not an OSHA fall-protection system. The old interim residential guidance that allowed them was rescinded.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(11), (b)(13); OSHA STD 03-11-002 (2010)

**3. Answer: C** — *Warning line — mechanical equipment*

When mechanical equipment is used, the warning line must be at least 6 ft from edges parallel to the direction of travel and at least 10 ft from edges perpendicular to it. The machine could run straight toward those edges.

- **A** is wrong: 6 ft is the distance for edges parallel to travel, or for work with no mechanical equipment.
- **B** is wrong: 8 ft is not a value in the warning-line rule.
- **D** is wrong: 15 ft exceeds the minimum. It is allowed, but it is not the required minimum.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(f)(1)(ii)

**4. Answer: B** — *Warning line construction*

The line's lowest point, including sag, must be no less than 34 in above the walking/working surface, and its highest point no more than 39 in. A 31-in sag is too low.

- **A** is wrong: Stanchions must resist at least 16 lb at 30 in, so 20 lb exceeds the requirement.
- **C** is wrong: Flagging is required at intervals of not more than 6 ft, so 5 ft complies.
- **D** is wrong: 38 in is within the 34–39 in range.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(f)(2)

**5. Answer: D** — *PFAS — anchorage location and free fall*

With the anchor at foot level, the worker falls the lanyard length plus the height of the back D-ring (about 5 ft) before the lanyard even starts to arrest: roughly 11 ft. A PFAS must be rigged so free fall is no more than 6 ft and the worker cannot contact a lower level. Fixes include an overhead or ridge anchor with a shorter connection, or a self-retracting lifeline or lanyard rated for foot-level anchorage.

- **A** is wrong: Lanyards are allowed on steep roofs when the PFAS is rigged correctly.
- **B** is wrong: A shock absorber limits the arresting force, but it does not cure excess free fall or the risk of hitting a lower level.
- **C** is wrong: 1,800 lb is the maximum arresting force on the worker, not the anchorage strength. Anchorage is 5,000 lb per worker, or designed with a safety factor of 2 by a qualified person.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(16)(iii); (d)(15)

**6. Answer: A** — *PFAS after a fall*

Components subjected to impact loading must be removed from service immediately. They may not be used again until a competent person inspects them and determines they are undamaged and suitable for reuse. Many manufacturers require destroying equipment that arrested a fall.

- **B** is wrong: The worker is not necessarily a competent person, and internal damage may not be visible.
- **C** is wrong: Continued use after an arrest is exactly what the rule prohibits.
- **D** is wrong: Harness webbing and stitching can also be damaged in an arrest.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(19)

**7. Answer: D** — *PFAS anchorage strength*

Anchorages for PFAS must support at least 5,000 lb per employee attached. The alternative is an anchorage designed, installed, and used as part of a complete PFAS with a safety factor of at least two, under the supervision of a qualified person.

- **A** is wrong: 1,800 lb is the maximum arresting force allowed on the body with a harness.
- **B** is wrong: 3,000 lb applies to self-retracting lifelines that limit free fall to 2 ft or less, and to positioning-device anchorages.
- **C** is wrong: 3,600 lb is the proof-test load for snaphooks and D-rings.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(15)

**8. Answer: B** — *Skylights as holes*

Skylights are treated as holes. Employees must be protected from falling through them by covers, guardrails, or PFAS. Covers must support at least twice the weight of the employees, equipment, and materials that may be imposed, and must be secured and marked.

- **A** is wrong: A skylight dome is not a cover, and falls through domes are a leading cause of roofing deaths.
- **C** is wrong: Marking a curb is a warning, not fall protection.
- **D** is wrong: Warning lines are not a permitted protection for holes.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(4); 1926.502(i)

**9. Answer: B** — *Material storage near roof edge*

During roofing work, materials and equipment may not be stored within 6 ft of a roof edge unless guardrails are erected at the edge.

- **A** is wrong: 4 ft is the storage distance used for overhand bricklaying, a different operation.
- **C** is wrong: 10 ft relates to warning lines perpendicular to mechanical equipment travel, not storage.
- **D** is wrong: 15 ft is not the rule, though storing farther back is permitted.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(j)

**10. Answer: C** — *Debris removal*

When material is dropped more than 20 ft to any point outside the exterior walls of a building, an enclosed chute must be used.

- **A** is wrong: Barricades (at least 42 in high and 6 ft back from the opening) apply when debris is dropped through holes in floors without chutes, not from roofs over 20 ft outside the walls.
- **B** is wrong: A spotter does not replace the chute requirement.
- **D** is wrong: Bagging does not change the drop-height rule.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.252(a)–(b)

**11. Answer: C** — *Ladder setup calculation*

The horizontal distance from the top support to the foot should be about one-quarter of the working length. That is commonly figured as one-quarter of the height to the support point: 20 ft ÷ 4 = 5 ft. The ladder rails must also extend 3 ft above the landing.

- **A** is wrong: 3 ft is too steep, and the ladder could tip backward.
- **B** is wrong: 4 ft would be correct for a 16-ft support height.
- **D** is wrong: 6 ft 8 in is a 3-to-1 ratio. That is too shallow, and the base could kick out.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(5)(i), (b)(1)

**12. Answer: C** — *Ladders near power*

Portable ladders must have nonconductive side rails if they are used where the employee or the ladder could contact exposed energized electrical equipment. Clearance from lines must still be maintained.

- **A** is wrong: Wood can conduct when wet or dirty. "Always safe" is false, and wood ladders are rarely used by roofers.
- **B** is wrong: Duty rating and tying off do not make aluminum nonconductive.
- **D** is wrong: Rubber feet do not insulate the rails. Current flows through the worker.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(12); 1926.416

**13. Answer: A** — *GFCI*

All 120-volt, single-phase, 15- and 20-ampere receptacle outlets on construction sites that are not part of the permanent wiring and are in use by employees must have GFCI protection, unless an assured equipment grounding conductor program is used.

- **B** is wrong: The GFCI rule is for 120-V single-phase 15/20-A receptacles, not 480-V disconnects.
- **C** is wrong: This charger is already on a GFCI outlet, so nothing more is required.
- **D** is wrong: The rule targets temporary receptacles used by employees, not the permanent wiring.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.404(b)(1)

**14. Answer: C** — *Overhead power line clearance*

For lines rated 50 kV or less, the commonly required minimum clearance for equipment and unqualified workers is 10 ft. For cranes and derricks, 1926.1408 Table A gives 10 ft up to 50 kV. When the voltage is unknown, crane rules use 20 ft. The best practice is to have the utility de-energize or insulate the line.

- **A** is wrong: 3 ft is far too close for an unqualified worker or equipment.
- **B** is wrong: 6 ft is not the clearance for 13.2 kV lines.
- **D** is wrong: 50 ft is more than required for this voltage, though larger clearances apply at very high voltages.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1408 Table A; 1910.333(c)(3); 1926.416(a)(1)

**15. Answer: D** — *Noise dose calculation*

Dose = C1/T1 + C2/T2 = 4/4 + 2/2 = 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0. Any sum greater than 1.0 exceeds the permissible exposure, so controls and hearing protection are required.

- **A** is wrong: 0.75 comes from using the wrong permissible times.
- **B** is wrong: 1.00 counts only one of the two exposures.
- **C** is wrong: 1.50 is an arithmetic error; each fraction equals 1.0.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.52(d) and Table D-2

**16. Answer: D** — *Respiratory protection*

Under the respiratory protection standard, the employer must provide a medical evaluation to determine the employee's ability to use a respirator before the employee is fit-tested or required to use it. Fit testing is then done before first use and at least annually.

- **A** is wrong: No experience period is required; training is required.
- **B** is wrong: HazCom training is separate and is not a prerequisite for fit testing.
- **C** is wrong: A chest X-ray is not the prerequisite for respirator use.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.103 → 1910.134(e), (f)

**17. Answer: C** — *Safety Data Sheets*

Section 9 lists physical and chemical properties, such as flash point, vapor pressure, and boiling point.

- **A** is wrong: Section 2 lists the hazard classification, signal word, pictograms, and hazard statements.
- **B** is wrong: Section 8 covers exposure limits, engineering controls, and PPE.
- **D** is wrong: Section 11 covers health effects and toxicity data.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1910.1200 Appendix D (via 1926.59)

**18. Answer: A** — *GHS label signal words*

Under the GHS-aligned Hazard Communication Standard, "Danger" is used for the more severe hazard categories and "Warning" for the less severe.

- **B** is wrong: "Caution" is not a GHS signal word.
- **C** is wrong: "Notice" is a sign term for non-hazard information, not a label signal word.
- **D** is wrong: "Warning" is the signal word for less severe hazards.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1910.1200(c) definitions ("signal word"); Appendix C

**19. Answer: C** — *Asbestos — before tear-off*

Determine whether asbestos is present before disturbing it. If it is, removal is Class II work that requires specific methods (intact removal, wet methods, no dropping or throwing, isolating HVAC intakes) and a competent person. Environmental notification rules may also apply.

- **A** is wrong: Wet methods are part of the procedure, but you can't pick the procedure until you know whether ACM is present. Nonfriable materials can become regulated when they are cut or abraded.
- **B** is wrong: Re-covering is limited by code and doesn't remove the need to identify ACM that will be disturbed during the work.
- **D** is wrong: Power cutting can release fibers. Cutting ACM roofing requires misting and specific controls.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1101(g)(8)(ii), (k); NRCA asbestos guide (paywalled; not read)

**20. Answer: D** — *Asbestos roofing removal methods*

Unwrapped or unbagged ACM roofing must be lowered by a covered, dust-tight chute, crane, or hoist, or it must be bagged or wrapped. It may never be dropped or thrown to the ground.

- **A** is wrong: Bagging or wrapping is an approved method.
- **B** is wrong: Continuous misting of cutting machines is required.
- **C** is wrong: A dust-tight chute is an approved method.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1101(g)(8)(ii)

**21. Answer: B** — *Kettle fire*

Closing the lid smothers the fire by cutting off oxygen. Shutting off the fuel removes the heat source. A B-rated extinguisher can handle any remaining flame.

- **A** is wrong: Stirring feeds oxygen and spreads the fire.
- **C** is wrong: Adding material near an open fire exposes the worker and does not stop combustion.
- **D** is wrong: Water on hot asphalt flashes to steam and sprays burning asphalt, making the fire far worse.

*Source:* NRCA safety practice (NRCA Safety Manual — paywalled; not read); 29 CFR 1926.150

**22. Answer: C** — *Torch-applied roofing fire watch*

CERTA requires a fire watch of at least 2 hours after torching ends, because smoldering fires in concealed spaces can take time to develop.

- **A** is wrong: 30 minutes is too short under CERTA.
- **B** is wrong: 1 hour appears in some fire codes, but CERTA requires 2 hours.
- **D** is wrong: The shift may end sooner than 2 hours after torching; the watch is time-based.

*Source:* NRCA CERTA Student Manual (public NRCA document)

**23. Answer: B** — *Heating asphalt*

Asphalt must never be heated to its flash point, where its vapors can ignite. Common practice keeps the kettle well below the flash point (often at least 25°F below). Application is done at the EVT ±25°F.

- **A** is wrong: The softening point (roughly 135–225°F) is far below application temperature and is necessarily exceeded.
- **C** is wrong: A fixed 350°F isn't the rule. Many asphalts are applied near or above 400°F, depending on their EVT.
- **D** is wrong: EVT is the target application temperature, so it must be reached.

*Source:* NRCA practice; asphalt bill of lading/SDS (flash point, EVT, FBT)

**24. Answer: B** — *Heat illness*

Confusion and hot, dry skin with little sweating are signs of heat stroke, a medical emergency. Call 911 and cool the worker aggressively (shade, ice or cold water, fanning) while waiting.

- **A** is wrong: Heat exhaustion typically shows heavy sweating and cool, clammy skin. Leaving a confused worker alone in a hot truck is dangerous.
- **C** is wrong: Heat cramps involve painful muscle spasms without confusion.
- **D** is wrong: Returning a confused worker to the roof ignores a life-threatening emergency.

*Source:* OSHA/NIOSH heat illness guidance; NRCA safety practice

**25. Answer: B** — *OSHA injury reporting*

Work-related in-patient hospitalizations, amputations, and losses of an eye must be reported to OSHA within 24 hours. Fatalities must be reported within 8 hours.

- **A** is wrong: 8 hours is the deadline for fatalities.
- **C** is wrong: 7 calendar days is the deadline for entering a recordable case on the OSHA 300 log.
- **D** is wrong: 30 days is not an OSHA reporting deadline.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1904.39; 1904.29


## Section 2: Roofing Preparation & Materials

**26. Answer: A** — *Material storage — roll goods*

NRCA practice is to store rolls on end to prevent flattening and deformation (which cause wrinkles and fishmouths), off the ground, and protected from moisture with breathable covers.

- **B** is wrong: Concentrated loads belong over structural supports, not mid-span.
- **C** is wrong: Factory wrap is for shipping, not weather protection. Rolls on the ground pick up moisture.
- **D** is wrong: Rolls stored flat deform.

*Source:* NRCA Roofing Manual practice (paywalled; not read); manufacturer storage instructions

**27. Answer: B** — *Insulation storage*

Insulation must be kept dry, off the ground, and covered with secured, breathable waterproof covers. Wet insulation loses R-value, its facers delaminate, and it can't be installed.

- **A** is wrong: The facers don't protect the board edges, and ground contact wicks water in.
- **C** is wrong: Factory wrap is not weather protection, and it often has openings or tears.
- **D** is wrong: Non-breathable plastic sealed to the ground traps condensation under the cover.

*Source:* NRCA practice; insulation manufacturer storage guidance

**28. Answer: C** — *Re-cover limitations*

The model codes prohibit a new covering over an existing one when two or more applications already exist, when the existing roof is water-soaked or deteriorated, or when it is slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile. NRCA also generally favors tear-off.

- **A** is wrong: An owner can't waive a code requirement.
- **B** is wrong: Longer nails don't overcome the code prohibition.
- **D** is wrong: Being dry doesn't matter when there are already two layers.

*Source:* IRC R908.3.1.1 / IBC 1511.3.1.1 (2018/2021) [JURISDICTION]

**29. Answer: A** — *Moisture survey confirmation*

Core cuts physically confirm the wetness and show the actual assembly. They are the standard verification for infrared, nuclear, and capacitance surveys. Patch each core immediately.

- **B** is wrong: Soft spots can point to wet areas, but walking alone is unreliable and doesn't measure moisture.
- **C** is wrong: Waiting for a leak lets damage continue and still doesn't locate the wet insulation.
- **D** is wrong: Midday sun heats the whole surface and masks the differences infrared relies on. Scans are done after sunset.

*Source:* NRCA practice (moisture surveys); ASTM C1153 (infrared imaging of roofs)

**30. Answer: D** — *LWIC deck base sheet*

LWIC contains water that must be able to escape. Base sheets (often venting base sheets) are mechanically fastened with LWIC-specific fasteners, per the deck and roofing manufacturers.

- **A** is wrong: An unattached base sheet provides no wind resistance or securement.
- **B** is wrong: Solid mopping traps moisture and can cause blistering and poor adhesion.
- **C** is wrong: Solvent contact adhesives are not the standard attachment to LWIC.

*Source:* NRCA practice; LWIC manufacturer instructions [MANUFACTURER]

**31. Answer: A** — *Insulation compatibility with hot asphalt*

Polystyrene (EPS and XPS) melts when it contacts hot asphalt, and solvents attack it. It needs a compatible cover board, or it has to be attached mechanically or with approved adhesives.

- **B** is wrong: Perlite is compatible with hot asphalt and is often used as a cover board over foam.
- **C** is wrong: Polyiso tolerates hot asphalt and is commonly set in it.
- **D** is wrong: Fiberboard is compatible with hot asphalt.

*Source:* Insulation manufacturer data; NRCA practice

**32. Answer: A** — *PVC over asphalt*

Asphalt and PVC are incompatible. Oils and plasticizers migrate, making the PVC brittle. A manufacturer-approved separation sheet or cover board is required.

- **B** is wrong: PVC is not compatible with asphalt.
- **C** is wrong: Adhering PVC directly in asphalt causes exactly the incompatibility problem.
- **D** is wrong: Priming the asphalt doesn't separate it from the PVC.

*Source:* PVC manufacturer requirements; NRCA practice

**33. Answer: D** — *Galvanic corrosion*

Runoff carrying copper ions attacks zinc (galvanizing) and aluminum, which are less noble. The gutter corrodes.

- **A** is wrong: The metals are dissimilar, and water provides the electrolyte.
- **B** is wrong: Staining can occur, but the real concern is metal loss in the gutter.
- **C** is wrong: Copper is the more noble metal and is protected in this pairing.

*Source:* Galvanic series; NRCA architectural metal practice

**34. Answer: D** — *Asphalt types*

Type IV ("special steep," about 210–225°F softening point) resists flow on steeper slopes and in hot climates. Type I (135–151°F) is the dead-level asphalt.

- **A** is wrong: Type I has the lowest softening point and is for dead-level roofs.
- **B** is wrong: Type II (158–176°F) is for low slopes.
- **C** is wrong: Type III (185–205°F) is the most common BUR asphalt, but not the highest.

*Source:* ASTM D312

**35. Answer: B** — *Primer on concrete*

Asphalt primer (ASTM D41) penetrates the concrete surface and binds surface dust, so hot asphalt bonds properly. It is also used on metal surfaces.

- **A** is wrong: Roof cement is a trowel-grade material, not a primer.
- **C** is wrong: Coal-tar products are incompatible with asphalt systems.
- **D** is wrong: Water must never be on a surface receiving hot asphalt; it causes steam blistering and poor bond.

*Source:* ASTM D41; NRCA practice

**36. Answer: D** — *Insulation R-value ranking*

Polyiso has the highest R per inch of common roof insulations (about 5.6–5.7 LTTR), though its performance drops in cold weather.

- **A** is wrong: Perlite is roughly R-2.6 to 2.8 per inch.
- **B** is wrong: Mineral wool is roughly R-3.7 to 4.2 per inch.
- **C** is wrong: EPS is roughly R-3.6 to 4.2 per inch.

*Source:* Manufacturer data (ASTM C1289, C578, C728, C726)

**37. Answer: A** — *Vapor retarder location*

A vapor retarder goes on the warm-in-winter side of the insulation. That keeps interior moisture from reaching surfaces colder than its dew point. In a heated building, that's below the insulation, on the deck side.

- **B** is wrong: A midpoint location can leave the lower layer's cold face below the dew point, depending on the design.
- **C** is wrong: A retarder under the membrane traps moisture inside the insulation, on the cold side.
- **D** is wrong: Location is critical.

*Source:* NRCA condensation guidance (paywalled; summary); building science practice

**38. Answer: A** — *Vapor retarder classes*

Class I is 0.1 perm or less (e.g., polyethylene sheet, foil). Class II is more than 0.1 up to 1.0. Class III is more than 1.0 up to 10.

- **B** is wrong: This range is Class II.
- **C** is wrong: This range is Class III.
- **D** is wrong: Above 10 perms, a material is considered vapor-permeable.

*Source:* IRC/IBC definitions (vapor retarder class) [JURISDICTION]

**39. Answer: C** — *Estimating bundles*

Plan area = 48 × 32 = 1,536 sq ft. Roof area = 1,536 × 1.202 = 1,846.3 sq ft. Add 10% → 2,030.9 sq ft = 20.31 squares → round up to 21 squares. 21 × 3 = 63 bundles.

- **A** is wrong: 57 bundles (19 squares) ignores the 10% waste.
- **B** is wrong: 60 bundles (20 squares) rounds down instead of up.
- **D** is wrong: 66 bundles (22 squares) over-rounds or adds extra waste.

*Source:* Standard estimating method (slope factor = √(rise² + 144)/12)

**40. Answer: C** — *Nails per square*

Shingles per square = 14,400 sq in ÷ (5 in × 36 in) = 80. 80 × 6 nails = 480 nails.

- **A** is wrong: 320 is the count at 4 nails per shingle.
- **B** is wrong: 400 corresponds to 5 nails per shingle.
- **D** is wrong: 540 would require 90 shingles per square, which is the wrong exposure math.

*Source:* Standard estimating method; manufacturer high-wind fastening

**41. Answer: D** — *Hip length*

Hip length = common run × hip factor = 14 × 1.5 = 21.0 ft. For 6:12, the factor is √(6² + 288)/12 = 18/12 = 1.5.

- **A** is wrong: 15.7 ft is the common-rafter length (14 × 1.118), not the hip.
- **B** is wrong: 18.0 ft misapplies the factor.
- **C** is wrong: 19.8 ft is 14 × √2, which ignores the rise.

*Source:* Roof framing geometry; standard estimating method

**42. Answer: D** — *Deck defect found during tear-off*

The roofer does not design or certify the structure but must not roof over an unsuitable deck. The best practice is to stop, document, and notify in writing, then proceed after an authorized repair (often a unit-price item).

- **A** is wrong: An unauthorized plywood patch is not an engineered steel deck repair and may not carry loads or hold fasteners.
- **B** is wrong: The roofer is expected to verify that the deck is suitable to receive roofing.
- **C** is wrong: Insulation doesn't restore structural capacity.

*Source:* NRCA practice (deck suitability); standard specification language [INFERENCE]

**43. Answer: A** — *Treated lumber and aluminum*

Copper-based treatments such as ACQ and CA are corrosive to aluminum and to light galvanizing. Isolate them with a separation membrane, or use compatible metals and fasteners (hot-dip galvanized or stainless).

- **B** is wrong: Aluminum does not rot wood.
- **C** is wrong: Modern copper-based treatments are chemically active.
- **D** is wrong: Shrinkage is not the compatibility issue this question describes.

*Source:* Treated-wood and fastener manufacturer guidance; IRC R317.3 [JURISDICTION]

**44. Answer: D** — *Dew point and condensation*

Water vapor condenses on any surface colder than the dew point of the air touching it. Controlling air leakage and vapor keeps moist interior air from reaching cold surfaces.

- **A** is wrong: Outdoor temperature alone doesn't decide it. What matters is the surface temperature compared with the dew point of the air reaching it.
- **B** is wrong: A surface warmer than the air can't collect condensation from that air.
- **C** is wrong: Above the dew point, there is no condensation.

*Source:* Psychrometrics; NRCA condensation guidance (paywalled)

**45. Answer: A** — *Rooftop material loading*

A full pallet can put about 200 psf on a small area, far more than typical roof design loads. Spreading bundles near bearing points, and straddling the ridge where the manufacturer allows, protects the structure and keeps the load balanced.

- **B** is wrong: Roofs are not designed for unlimited concentrated construction loads.
- **C** is wrong: Concentrated mid-span loads can overload the rafters or trusses.
- **D** is wrong: Stacking at the eave concentrates the load on the overhang and creates a falling-object hazard at the edge.

*Source:* NRCA material handling practice; manufacturer loading guidance


## Section 3: Steep-Slope Roofing Systems

**46. Answer: B** — *Minimum slope — asphalt shingles*

Asphalt shingles may be installed on slopes of 2:12 or greater. Slopes from 2:12 up to less than 4:12 need special (double) underlayment.

- **A** is wrong: 1:12 is the minimum for mineral-surfaced roll roofing, not shingles.
- **C** is wrong: 3:12 is the minimum for wood shingles, wood shakes, and metal shingles.
- **D** is wrong: 4:12 is where normal single-layer underlayment applies, and it is the slate minimum.

*Source:* IRC R905.2.2 [JURISDICTION]; NRCA Steep-slope manual (paywalled)

**47. Answer: D** — *Low-slope shingle underlayment*

For slopes from 2:12 up to less than 4:12, double coverage is required. Begin with a 19-in-wide strip at the eave, then lap each 36-in sheet 19 in over the previous one, so every point has two layers.

- **A** is wrong: Underlayment is required regardless of shingle type.
- **B** is wrong: A single layer with 2-in laps is the method for 4:12 and steeper.
- **C** is wrong: A single layer with a wider lap is still only one layer in most places.

*Source:* IRC R905.1.1 [JURISDICTION]; manufacturer low-slope instructions

**48. Answer: B** — *Eave sequence*

At the eave, the drip edge goes on the deck first and the underlayment laps over its flange. Water running down the underlayment then lands on the metal and drips clear.

- **A** is wrong: Underlayment goes under the shingles, never over them.
- **C** is wrong: This is the sequence for the RAKE, not the eave. At the eave it lets water get behind the flange.
- **D** is wrong: The starter strip goes on top of the underlayment and drip edge.

*Source:* IRC R905.2.8.5 [JURISDICTION]; manufacturer instructions

**49. Answer: A** — *Rake sequence*

At rakes, the underlayment is installed first and the drip edge laps over it. That keeps wind-driven rain from getting under the underlayment edge.

- **B** is wrong: Drip edge under the underlayment is the EAVE method.
- **C** is wrong: Shingles overlap the drip edge; the metal is never placed on top of them.
- **D** is wrong: Code and manufacturers call for drip edge at both eaves and rakes.

*Source:* IRC R905.2.8.5 [JURISDICTION]

**50. Answer: B** — *Ice barrier extent*

The ice barrier (two layers of underlayment cemented together, or a self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen sheet) extends from the lowest edges of all roof surfaces to a point at least 24 in inside the exterior wall line.

- **A** is wrong: Stopping 12 in past the fascia leaves the area over the heated space unprotected.
- **C** is wrong: Ice dams back water up over the heated space, beyond the wall line.
- **D** is wrong: A fixed 36-in width may fall short on wide overhangs or steep slopes.

*Source:* IRC R905.1.2 [JURISDICTION]

**51. Answer: C** — *Ice barrier calculation*

Horizontal distance = 18 + 24 = 42 in. Along the slope: 42 × 1.158 = 48.6 in. A single 36-in-wide ice-barrier course is not enough; you need two courses.

- **A** is wrong: 42.0 in is the horizontal distance, not measured along the slope.
- **B** is wrong: 44.4 in uses the wrong slope factor.
- **D** is wrong: 52.0 in adds extra width that isn't required.

*Source:* IRC R905.1.2 [JURISDICTION]; slope-factor geometry

**52. Answer: B** — *Shingle blow-off diagnosis*

When nails are placed above the manufacturer's nailing zone, they don't pass through the shingle below, so each shingle has half the intended holding power. Combined with sealant strips that haven't sealed (common with fall installation), this is the classic cause of blow-offs.

- **A** is wrong: Starter strips improve wind resistance at the eave.
- **C** is wrong: Using one production lot prevents color shading and doesn't affect wind resistance.
- **D** is wrong: Poor ventilation causes curling and premature aging, not rows of blow-offs with high nail holes.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions; NRCA practice

**53. Answer: B** — *Nail pops*

Underdriven nails, or nails backing out of a deck that shrinks and moves, lift the shingle above. That prevents sealing and eventually tears the shingle.

- **A** is wrong: Galvanized nails are the standard corrosion-resistant fastener.
- **C** is wrong: Overdriven nails cause cut-through and blow-offs, not raised heads.
- **D** is wrong: The fastener count doesn't cause pops.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions; NRCA practice

**54. Answer: C** — *Fasteners per shingle*

The code minimum is 4 fasteners per strip shingle (2 per individual shingle). Manufacturers typically require 6 for high-wind areas and very steep slopes.

- **A** is wrong: Two fasteners is the minimum for individual (not strip) shingles.
- **B** is wrong: Three is not a code or manufacturer standard.
- **D** is wrong: Six is the high-wind/steep-slope requirement, not the standard minimum.

*Source:* IRC R905.2.6 [JURISDICTION]; manufacturer instructions

**55. Answer: C** — *Very steep slopes (mansard)*

Above about 21:12, sealant strips alone may not hold because gravity pulls the shingles away. Manufacturers call for extra nails (typically 6) and hand-sealing with spots of roof cement.

- **A** is wrong: Higher nailing reduces holding power; nails must stay in the nailing zone.
- **B** is wrong: Very steep slopes need special fastening.
- **D** is wrong: Double underlayment is the rule for LOW slopes (2:12 to below 4:12).

*Source:* Manufacturer steep-slope application instructions

**56. Answer: D** — *Cold-weather installation*

Sealant strips need heat from the sun to bond. In cold weather, the shingles stay unsealed and vulnerable to wind until they seal. Hand-sealing gives immediate resistance.

- **A** is wrong: Nails above the nailing zone miss the underlying course and reduce holding power.
- **B** is wrong: Sealant strips are heat-activated.
- **C** is wrong: Covering the roof with plastic is not a practical or recommended method.

*Source:* Manufacturer cold-weather application instructions

**57. Answer: D** — *Valley type with laminated shingles*

Laminated shingles are thick and stiff and don't bend smoothly through a woven valley. The result is bridging and voids. Closed-cut or open valleys are used instead.

- **A** is wrong: This is an open metal valley, which is appropriate.
- **B** is wrong: An open metal valley is appropriate for any shingle type.
- **C** is wrong: Closed-cut is the most common valley for laminated shingles.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions

**58. Answer: D** — *Closed-cut valley sequence*

Manufacturers generally have the lower-slope (or smaller) plane's shingles run across the valley first. The steeper plane's faster-flowing water then runs over the cut edge, not into it.

- **A** is wrong: That reverses the recommended order and sends the steeper plane's water against the cut edge.
- **B** is wrong: Weaving is a different valley method and isn't recommended for laminated shingles.
- **C** is wrong: Cutting both sides makes an open valley, which needs a metal or roll-roofing liner.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions [MANUFACTURER]

**59. Answer: A** — *Closed-cut valley details*

The cut side is trimmed about 2 in back from the centerline. The upper corner is clipped so water isn't directed under the shingle, and the edge is set in cement. No fasteners go within about 6 in of the centerline, where the most water flows.

- **B** is wrong: Folding the shingles doesn't create a closed-cut valley.
- **C** is wrong: Nails in the valley center are a classic cause of valley leaks.
- **D** is wrong: 6 in is the distance to keep nails from the centerline, not the cut line, and the cement is part of the method.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions [MANUFACTURER]

**60. Answer: C** — *Open metal valley*

The model code table for valley linings requires metal valley flashing at least 24 in wide.

- **A** is wrong: 12 in is too narrow to carry the valley's water.
- **B** is wrong: 16 in is too narrow for code metal valley linings.
- **D** is wrong: 36 in is the common width for the valley UNDERLAYMENT, not the metal lining.

*Source:* IRC Table R905.2.8.2 [JURISDICTION]

**61. Answer: D** — *Step flashing attachment*

Fastening the roof leg only, near the top where the next shingle covers it, lets the roof and wall move independently. The fastener stays covered, and the vertical leg stays free.

- **A** is wrong: Nailing both legs locks the flashing and invites cracking and leaks.
- **B** is wrong: Nailing the wall leg ties the flashing to the wall and can tear it as the structures move.
- **C** is wrong: Sealant alone isn't a reliable attachment.

*Source:* NRCA steep-slope flashing practice; manufacturer instructions

**62. Answer: C** — *Sidewall flashing error*

Step flashing is woven into each course, so each piece sheds water onto the shingle below it. A continuous L-flashing carries water along its length and dumps it under the shingle laps.

- **A** is wrong: Condensation is unrelated to this flashing error.
- **B** is wrong: Heat is irrelevant here.
- **D** is wrong: Thickness is not the issue.

*Source:* NRCA steep-slope flashing practice

**63. Answer: B** — *Kickout flashing*

Where a roof-wall intersection ends before the wall does, a kickout flashing diverts water off the wall and into the gutter. Without it, water runs behind the cladding and rots the wall.

- **A** is wrong: Ice barrier at the rake doesn't address water at the bottom of a sidewall.
- **C** is wrong: Crickets divert water around chimneys and wide penetrations, not at sidewall eaves.
- **D** is wrong: A ridge vent doesn't affect water at a sidewall-eave intersection.

*Source:* IRC R903.2.1 [JURISDICTION]; NRCA practice

**64. Answer: B** — *Chimney cricket*

A cricket or saddle is required on the ridge side of any chimney or penetration more than 30 in wide, measured perpendicular to the slope. It diverts water and debris around the chimney.

- **A** is wrong: Step flashing alone leaves a dam where water and debris collect behind the chimney.
- **C** is wrong: Sealant is not a substitute for a cricket.
- **D** is wrong: Pitch pans are low-slope membrane details, and a last resort even there.

*Source:* IRC R903.2.2, R1003.20 [JURISDICTION]

**65. Answer: A** — *Chimney counterflashing*

The chimney and the roof framing move differently through settlement, thermal movement, and shrinkage. The base flashing is fastened to the roof, and the counterflashing to the masonry. The lap lets them slide without tearing.

- **B** is wrong: The counterflashing does not hold shingles.
- **C** is wrong: Fire resistance is not the reason for the two-part system.
- **D** is wrong: Flashing carries no structural load.

*Source:* NRCA practice (chimney flashing)

**66. Answer: B** — *Plumbing vent flashing*

Following the water-shedding rule, water coming down the roof flows over the shingles above and onto the flange, then off the flange onto the shingles below. It never flows under a lap.

- **A** is wrong: The flange must integrate with the shingles, not sit under the underlayment.
- **C** is wrong: A flange fully on top lets water run under its upper edge.
- **D** is wrong: A flange fully under the shingles traps water at its lower edge and directs it under the courses below.

*Source:* NRCA practice; manufacturer instructions

**67. Answer: A** — *Mixing exhaust vents*

Different exhaust vents in one attic short-circuit each other. The fan or a gable vent can pull air in through the ridge vent, drawing in snow and rain. Balanced soffit intake with one exhaust type is recommended.

- **B** is wrong: A second fan worsens the short-circuiting.
- **C** is wrong: More gable louvers add to the short-circuiting.
- **D** is wrong: Sealing the soffits removes the intake and makes the exhaust vents pull air (and weather) in through the ridge.

*Source:* Shingle and ventilation manufacturer guidance; NRCA ventilation practice

**68. Answer: C** — *Ventilation calculation*

Required NFA = 2,100 ÷ 300 = 7 sq ft = 1,008 sq in. Half is exhaust: 504 sq in. 504 ÷ 18 = 28 linear feet.

- **A** is wrong: 14 ft is half the correct number, from splitting the exhaust twice.
- **B** is wrong: 24 ft comes from a different attic area.
- **D** is wrong: 56 ft puts all of the required NFA at the ridge.

*Source:* IRC R806.2 [JURISDICTION]; manufacturer NFA ratings

**69. Answer: D** — *Ice dams*

Ice dams form when heat leaking into the attic melts snow, and the meltwater refreezes at the cold eave. Air sealing, insulation, and ventilation prevent the dam. An ice barrier reduces damage but doesn't stop dams from forming.

- **A** is wrong: Extra shingle layers don't stop the dam, and re-cover limits may apply.
- **B** is wrong: Shingle weight doesn't change the heat loss that causes the dam.
- **C** is wrong: Removing gutters doesn't address the cause.

*Source:* NRCA condensation/ventilation guidance; building science practice

**70. Answer: A** — *Tile minimum slope*

Clay and concrete tile require a minimum slope of 2½:12. Between 2½:12 and 4:12, the code requires double underlayment.

- **B** is wrong: A single underlayment layer is for 4:12 and steeper.
- **C** is wrong: 4:12 is the minimum for slate, not tile.
- **D** is wrong: 6:12 is not the tile minimum.

*Source:* IRC R905.3.2, R905.3.3 [JURISDICTION]

**71. Answer: B** — *Tile — converting from asphalt*

Concrete and clay tile weigh several times more than asphalt shingles (often about 9–12 psf versus about 2–4 psf). The structure must be evaluated before the conversion.

- **A** is wrong: Aesthetics are secondary to structural adequacy.
- **C** is wrong: Ventilation is secondary to structure for this decision.
- **D** is wrong: Gutter size is a secondary concern.

*Source:* Tile manufacturer guidance; NRCA practice

**72. Answer: B** — *Slate exposure calculation*

Slate exposure = (length − headlap) ÷ 2 = (24 − 3) ÷ 2 = 10.5 in.

- **A** is wrong: 9.0 in subtracts too much.
- **C** is wrong: 12.0 in is half the length, which ignores the headlap.
- **D** is wrong: 21.0 in forgets to divide by 2. Each course must be covered by the two courses above it.

*Source:* NRCA slate practice (Steep-slope manual — paywalled); standard slate formula

**73. Answer: B** — *Slate fastening*

Slate lasts for generations and needs fasteners that last as long, such as copper or stainless steel. Driving nails tight cracks the slate. It should hang on the nails.

- **A** is wrong: Staples are not used for slate.
- **C** is wrong: Slate requires two nails.
- **D** is wrong: Electro-galvanized nails corrode long before the slate wears out, and tight nailing cracks slate.

*Source:* NRCA slate practice; slate industry guidance

**74. Answer: C** — *Slate grades*

ASTM C406 grades roofing slate as S1 (service life over 75 years), S2 (40–75), and S3 (20–40).

- **A** is wrong: 20–40 years is Grade S3.
- **B** is wrong: 40–75 years is Grade S2.
- **D** is wrong: 10–20 years is not an ASTM C406 grade.

*Source:* ASTM C406

**75. Answer: A** — *Wood shake interlayment*

Shakes are split and irregular, so they need an 18-in-wide No. 30 felt interlayment between courses. It is positioned above the butt a distance of twice the exposure, to block wind-driven rain.

- **B** is wrong: Foil is not the specified interlayment.
- **C** is wrong: Shakes are not self-sealing; the interlayment is required.
- **D** is wrong: A full self-adhered membrane under each course would trap moisture and isn't the standard method.

*Source:* IRC R905.8.7 [JURISDICTION]; Cedar Shake & Shingle Bureau practice

**76. Answer: B** — *Wood shingle spacing*

Wood shingles are spaced ¼ to ⅜ in apart to allow for swelling when wet. Shakes use ⅜ to ⅝ in.

- **A** is wrong: Tight shingles buckle when they swell.
- **C** is wrong: ⅜ to ⅝ in is the spacing for shakes.
- **D** is wrong: 1 to 1½ in is too wide. 1½ in is the minimum side-joint OFFSET between courses.

*Source:* IRC R905.7 [JURISDICTION]; CSSB practice

**77. Answer: C** — *Metal roof minimum slopes*

Lapped, non-soldered seam metal panels without lap sealant need at least 3:12. With applied lap sealant, the minimum is ½:12. Standing-seam panels can go down to ¼:12.

- **A** is wrong: ¼:12 is the standing-seam minimum.
- **B** is wrong: ½:12 applies when lap sealant is used.
- **D** is wrong: 4:12 is not the metal-panel threshold.

*Source:* IRC R905.10.2 [JURISDICTION]

**78. Answer: B** — *Thermal movement calculation*

ΔL = α × L × ΔT = 9.4 × 10⁻⁶ × 360 in × 120°F = 0.406 in, or about 0.41 in. Details and clips must accommodate this movement.

- **A** is wrong: 0.28 in uses the steel coefficient (6.5 × 10⁻⁶), which gives 0.281 in.
- **C** is wrong: 0.55 in comes from using the wrong length or temperature swing.
- **D** is wrong: 0.81 in is double the correct value.

*Source:* Thermal expansion formula; published coefficients of expansion

**79. Answer: A** — *Cutting coated metal panels*

Abrasive cutting throws hot metal particles that burn the paint and zinc coating and embed in nearby panels, where they rust. Shears and nibblers cut cleanly without heat. Clean up the swarf the same day.

- **B** is wrong: A torch destroys the coating and the panel edge.
- **C** is wrong: PPE protects the worker, not the panel.
- **D** is wrong: Abrasive wheels damage the coating and cause rust staining.

*Source:* Metal panel manufacturer instructions; NRCA metal practice

**80. Answer: A** — *Tile underlayment role*

Tile sheds most water, but wind-driven rain and snow get past it. The underlayment is the true waterproofing layer, so it must be durable, correctly lapped, and properly flashed.

- **B** is wrong: Battens hold the tile; they don't waterproof anything.
- **C** is wrong: Ridge mortar only secures and closes the ridge.
- **D** is wrong: Tile is water-shedding, not watertight.

*Source:* Tile industry and NRCA tile practice


## Section 4: Low-Slope Roofing Systems

**81. Answer: B** — *Low-slope minimum slope*

Most membrane systems require a minimum slope of ¼:12 (2%) for positive drainage. Coal-tar BUR is the exception, with a ⅛:12 minimum.

- **A** is wrong: ⅛:12 applies to coal-tar built-up roofs only.
- **C** is wrong: ½:12 is more than the minimum.
- **D** is wrong: 1:12 is more than the minimum.

*Source:* IBC 1507 [JURISDICTION]; NRCA Membrane manual (paywalled)

**82. Answer: C** — *Ponding definition*

NRCA defines ponding as water that remains on the roof surface 48 hours after the end of a rain, under conditions conducive to drying.

- **A** is wrong: 12 hours is too short.
- **B** is wrong: 24 hours is not the NRCA benchmark.
- **D** is wrong: 7 days is not the definition.

*Source:* NRCA definition (Membrane manual; widely published)

**83. Answer: A** — *EPDM seams*

EPDM is a thermoset rubber, so it can't be re-melted or welded. Seams use primer and factory seam tape, rolled with a silicone roller.

- **B** is wrong: Hot-air welding is for thermoplastics (TPO, PVC).
- **C** is wrong: Torching is for modified bitumen.
- **D** is wrong: Asphalt is incompatible with EPDM.

*Source:* EPDM manufacturer instructions; NRCA Membrane practice

**84. Answer: B** — *Fully adhered EPDM*

Contact adhesives work by coating both surfaces and letting the solvent flash off until the adhesive is tacky but won't string. Then the surfaces are mated.

- **A** is wrong: A wet, one-sided application traps solvent and bonds poorly (blistering).
- **C** is wrong: One side only is not how contact adhesive works.
- **D** is wrong: Bead application is for low-rise foam insulation adhesive, not EPDM bonding adhesive.

*Source:* EPDM manufacturer instructions

**85. Answer: D** — *TPO weld test*

In a properly fused thermoplastic seam, the weld is stronger than the membrane. The peel sample fails in the membrane (film tearing or exposed scrim), not at the interface.

- **A** is wrong: Clean separation at the interface is a cold (unfused) weld.
- **B** is wrong: Weld width must meet the manufacturer's minimum, commonly about 1½ in; ½ in is too narrow.
- **C** is wrong: Browning and scorching suggest a burnt weld from too much heat.

*Source:* TPO manufacturer welding instructions; NRCA practice

**86. Answer: C** — *When to test weld*

Welding parameters depend on ambient and membrane conditions. Test welds at the start of the day and after any change keep the welder settings right.

- **A** is wrong: Testing only after a failure is reactive and lets bad seams get installed.
- **B** is wrong: Weekly testing misses daily condition changes.
- **D** is wrong: Conditions change daily, so a one-time test isn't enough.

*Source:* Thermoplastic membrane manufacturer instructions

**87. Answer: C** — *BUR blister cause*

BUR blisters form where air or moisture is trapped in voids between plies. Sun heat expands it and lifts the plies. Complete moppings, brooming, and dry materials prevent them.

- **A** is wrong: Aggregate protects the roof and doesn't cause interply blisters.
- **B** is wrong: Type III is the most common BUR asphalt.
- **D** is wrong: Slope is linked to slippage, not blistering.

*Source:* NRCA Membrane manual practice (paywalled); Repair Manual (paywalled)

**88. Answer: D** — *BUR slippage*

Slippage happens when interply asphalt softens and flows. Causes include too low a softening point (the wrong asphalt type), too much asphalt, overheating (which lowers the softening point), and a lack of mechanical backnailing on steeper slopes.

- **A** is wrong: Insulation thickness doesn't cause slippage.
- **B** is wrong: A thin flood coat causes aggregate loss, not ply slippage.
- **C** is wrong: The ply count doesn't cause slippage.

*Source:* NRCA BUR practice; ASTM D312

**89. Answer: D** — *Cant strips*

Bituminous plies and flashings crack if bent at a sharp 90° angle, so cants provide a gradual transition. Single-ply membranes are flexible enough to turn 90° without a cant.

- **A** is wrong: Shingle sidewalls use step flashing, not cant strips.
- **B** is wrong: TPO and PVC do not need cant strips.
- **C** is wrong: EPDM does not need cant strips.

*Source:* NRCA Membrane manual details (paywalled)

**90. Answer: C** — *Base flashing height*

NRCA recommends base flashings at least 8 in above the finished roof surface, to stay above ponding, snow, and splash.

- **A** is wrong: 4 in is the typical counterflashing lap, not the base flashing height.
- **B** is wrong: 6 in is below NRCA's recommendation.
- **D** is wrong: 12 in exceeds the minimum.

*Source:* NRCA Membrane manual (paywalled; widely published)

**91. Answer: A** — *Pitch pans*

Pitch pans depend on sealant, which shrinks and separates over time. NRCA considers them a last resort, used only when a curb or a prefabricated boot can't be used. Fill them with pourable sealer crowned to shed water, and inspect them regularly.

- **B** is wrong: Round pipes are better flashed with prefabricated boots or flanged collars.
- **C** is wrong: Pitch pans need ongoing maintenance.
- **D** is wrong: Coatings are not pourable sealers and won't fill a pan properly.

*Source:* NRCA Membrane manual details (paywalled; widely published)

**92. Answer: A** — *APP modified bitumen*

APP is a plastomeric modifier, and APP sheets are typically torch-applied. Some cold-applied APP products exist. SBS sheets are commonly hot-mopped, cold-applied, self-adhered, or torched.

- **B** is wrong: Hot-air welding is a thermoplastic single-ply method.
- **C** is wrong: Mod-bit is adhered or torched, not ballasted.
- **D** is wrong: APP is generally not hot-mopped. Hot mopping is typical of SBS.

*Source:* Mod-bit manufacturer instructions; NRCA practice

**93. Answer: D** — *Torch-applied laps*

A consistent small bleed-out shows the bitumen reached flowing temperature and fused across the lap. No bleed-out suggests an unfused lap. Excessive bleed-out suggests overheating.

- **A** is wrong: Overheating shows as excessive bleed-out and scorching, not a uniform ¼–½ in.
- **B** is wrong: Too little heat shows as no bleed-out.
- **C** is wrong: Voids are indicated by a missing bleed-out.

*Source:* Mod-bit manufacturer instructions; CERTA practice

**94. Answer: D** — *Grease exposure*

PVC membranes, especially grease- and chemical-resistant formulations, resist fats and oils better than EPDM and asphaltic membranes. Grease containment at the fan is still recommended.

- **A** is wrong: Asphalt is softened by oils and greases.
- **B** is wrong: Asphaltic mod-bit is also attacked by grease.
- **C** is wrong: EPDM swells and degrades with oils and fats.

*Source:* Membrane manufacturer chemical-resistance data

**95. Answer: B** — *Leak water testing*

Starting low and working up, one component at a time, means that when a leak appears, the water came from the area just tested, not from upslope water running down.

- **A** is wrong: Interior observation helps, but it doesn't isolate the leak source on the roof.
- **C** is wrong: Flooding from the top wets everything downslope and makes it impossible to isolate the leak.
- **D** is wrong: Random testing gives ambiguous results.

*Source:* NRCA Repair Manual practice (paywalled); industry leak-investigation practice

**108. Answer: A** — *Advertising violations*

Knowingly failing to display the license number and name in advertising is a Class A misdemeanor with a $1,000 fine. Each day an advertisement runs is a separate offense, and administrative discipline can also apply.

- **B** is wrong: $250 is the minimum for the vehicle-display violation.
- **C** is wrong: The statute provides criminal penalties, not just warnings.

**109. Answer: D** — *Maximum discipline fine*

P.A. 104-427 raised the maximum disciplinary fine to $15,000 per violation. It was previously $10,000.

- **A** is wrong: $1,000 is the advertising misdemeanor fine and the top of the vehicle penalty range.

**110. Answer: A** — *Grounds for discipline — leakage*

Section 9.1 lists professional incompetence or gross negligence as grounds for discipline. Prima facie evidence may be a conviction or judgment relating to roof construction or repair that results in leakage within 90 days after the work is completed.

- **B** is wrong: A leak 10 years later is not the 90-day standard.

**111. Answer: C** — *Margin pricing*

Price = cost ÷ (1 − margin) = 32,000 ÷ 0.80 = $40,000. Check: profit of $8,000 ÷ $40,000 = 20%.

- **A** is wrong: $38,400 is a 20% MARKUP on cost, which is only a 16.7% margin.

*Source:* Standard financial management (NASCLA-type content)

**112. Answer: C** — *Current ratio*

**113. Answer: B** — *FUTA calculation*

FUTA is 6.0% of the first $7,000 per employee, less up to a 5.4% credit, for a net 0.6%. 0.006 × $7,000 = $42 per employee; × 12 = $504.

**114. Answer: C** — *Form I-9 timing*

**115. Answer: A** — *Payment bonds*

A payment bond guarantees that subcontractors and suppliers get paid. That matters on public projects, where liens usually can't be placed on public property. A performance bond protects the owner against contractor default.

## Section 6: Non-Residential Roofing Systems

**116. Answer: C** — *FM uplift rating*

FM Approvals uplift classes (1-60, 1-75, 1-90, and so on) indicate the uplift pressure, in psf, the assembly withstood in testing. "Class 1" refers to the fire and wind classification.

- **A** is wrong: It isn't a slope.
- **B** is wrong: It isn't an insulation value.
- **D** is wrong: The number is not a fire-endurance time.

*Source:* FM Approvals / FM Global Data Sheets 1-28, 1-29

**117. Answer: A** — *Wind zones*

Wind uplift is highest at corners, next highest along perimeters, and lowest in the field. Attachment is increased in corner and perimeter zones.

- **B** is wrong: Drains are not a wind zone.
- **C** is wrong: The field has the lowest design pressure.
- **D** is wrong: Perimeters are high, but corners are higher.

*Source:* ASCE 7; FM Global Data Sheet 1-28

**118. Answer: A** — *Fastener back-out*

When screws don't engage the deck properly, or cyclic deck movement and vibration work them loose, they back out and push up through the insulation and into the membrane.

- **B** is wrong: Adhesive quantity doesn't push fasteners up.
- **C** is wrong: UV damages the surface. It does not push fasteners up.
- **D** is wrong: Ponding causes other damage, not tenting at fasteners.

*Source:* NRCA practice; fastener manufacturer requirements (penetration through steel deck)

**119. Answer: D** — *Insulation adhesive*

Low-rise foam adhesives are widely used to adhere insulation to concrete and other substrates. Bead spacing is set by the manufacturer and the wind zone.

- **A** is wrong: Staples can't attach insulation to concrete.
- **B** is wrong: Paint is not an adhesive.
- **C** is wrong: Loose-laid insulation needs ballast.

*Source:* Insulation adhesive manufacturer instructions

**120. Answer: D** — *Natatorium*

Pools create very high interior humidity. A continuous, sealed vapor retarder on the warm side keeps moisture out of the insulation. Over steel, it goes on a substrate board. Air leaks at the edges and penetrations must be sealed.

- **A** is wrong: Reflectivity doesn't address the moisture drive.
- **B** is wrong: Fasteners don't control vapor, and they add penetrations through the vapor retarder.
- **C** is wrong: Ballast doesn't control vapor.

*Source:* NRCA condensation guidance (paywalled); building science practice

**121. Answer: D** — *Cold storage*

Vapor moves from warm, moist air toward cold, dry air. In cold storage the exterior is warmer and wetter, so the vapor retarder function belongs on the exterior (warm) side, often the membrane itself.

- **A** is wrong: A large temperature difference creates a strong vapor drive.
- **B** is wrong: That's the normal direction for a heated building, which is reversed here.
- **C** is wrong: Vapor drive follows temperature and vapor pressure, not a fixed direction.

*Source:* Building science practice; NRCA condensation guidance (paywalled)

**122. Answer: B** — *Structural standing seam clips*

Long metal panels move significantly with temperature. Sliding clips let the panel move while holding it down. A designated fixed point controls where movement starts.

- **A** is wrong: Systems still have a fixed point; sliding clips work together with it.
- **C** is wrong: Clips don't affect the fire rating.
- **D** is wrong: End laps are still needed on long runs, per the manufacturer.

*Source:* Metal panel manufacturer design guides; NRCA Metal Panel manual (paywalled)

**123. Answer: D** — *Metal building insulation*

The facing is the vapor retarder, so it goes on the warm-in-winter side, facing the interior. Its seams are sealed or taped so moist interior air can't reach the cold metal panel and condense.

- **A** is wrong: Removing the facing removes the vapor control.
- **B** is wrong: Metal buildings are prone to condensation.
- **C** is wrong: Facing the cold side traps moisture in the insulation.

*Source:* NAIMA / metal building insulation guidance; building science practice

**124. Answer: D** — *SPF surface texture*

Smooth, orange-peel, and coarse orange-peel textures are acceptable for coating. Popcorn and tree-bark textures aren't, because coatings can't fully cover the voids and water collects. The surface must be corrected.

- **A** is wrong: Texture is a key SPF quality criterion.
- **B** is wrong: Gravel doesn't fix a defective foam surface.
- **C** is wrong: Extra coating can't reliably fill deep voids.

*Source:* SPF industry guidance; manufacturer specifications; NRCA SPF manual (paywalled)

**125. Answer: B** — *SPF weather conditions*

Moisture on the substrate ruins adhesion and causes blistering. Most manufacturers require the surface to be dry and at least about 5°F above the dew point.

- **A** is wrong: Clean, dry concrete is a suitable substrate.
- **C** is wrong: 70°F with light wind is generally acceptable.
- **D** is wrong: Overcast, dry conditions don't by themselves prevent spraying.

*Source:* SPF manufacturer application guidance [MANUFACTURER]

**126. Answer: C** — *Coating calculation*

Wet mils needed = 15 ÷ 0.50 = 30 wet mils. Gallons per square = 30 ÷ 16 ≈ 1.875. For 80 squares: 1.875 × 80 = 150 gal.

- **A** is wrong: 75 gal treats dry mils as wet mils (15 ÷ 16 × 80).
- **B** is wrong: 120 gal assumes 1.5 gal per square.
- **D** is wrong: 240 gal assumes 3 gal per square.

*Source:* Coating calculation (1 gal = 231 cu in; 231 ÷ 14,400 sq in ≈ 0.016 in = 16 mils)

**127. Answer: B** — *Protected membrane roof*

In a PMR, the insulation is over the membrane and gets periodically wet. XPS is used because of its low water absorption. It is then covered with fabric and ballast or pavers.

- **A** is wrong: Faced polyiso absorbs water and its facers degrade when wet.
- **C** is wrong: Fiberboard absorbs water and would fail.
- **D** is wrong: Perlite absorbs water and would fail.

*Source:* PMR manufacturer and NRCA practice

**128. Answer: A** — *Waterproofing vs dampproofing*

Waterproofing is designed to resist water under hydrostatic head. Dampproofing only retards moisture and vapor where there is no hydrostatic pressure.

- **B** is wrong: They are different in performance and use.
- **C** is wrong: Both are usually applied on the positive (exterior) side. Location isn't the distinction.
- **D** is wrong: Waterproofing is used below grade and on plaza decks, as well as on roofs.

*Source:* NRCA Waterproofing Manual (2005, paywalled; not read); industry definitions

**129. Answer: B** — *Occupied building fumes*

Asphalt fumes and odors drawn into air intakes can affect occupants, especially in sensitive facilities. Coordinated intake shutdown or sealing, scheduling, and low-fume equipment are standard practice.

- **A** is wrong: Hotter asphalt produces more fumes and raises the fire risk.
- **C** is wrong: Open hatches let fumes into the building.
- **D** is wrong: Working without owner coordination risks exposing occupants.

*Source:* NRCA practice; owner/facility coordination practice [INFERENCE]

**130. Answer: A** — *Rooftop equipment supports*

NRCA publishes minimum clearances that increase with equipment width, because a roofer must reach farther under wider units to install and repair flashings and membrane.

- **B** is wrong: Setting equipment on the membrane damages it and blocks reroofing.
- **C** is wrong: One fixed small clearance doesn't allow for wide units.
- **D** is wrong: Clearance is driven by maintenance and reroofing access, not by minimizing height.

*Source:* NRCA Membrane manual (equipment support guidance; paywalled; principle widely published)
