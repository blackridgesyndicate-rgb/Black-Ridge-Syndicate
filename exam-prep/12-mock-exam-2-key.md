# Mock Exam 2 — Answer Key with Explanations


## Section 1: Safety & Employee Protection

**1. Answer: B** — *Low-slope roof wider than 50 ft*

On a low-slope roof wider than 50 ft, a safety monitor alone isn't permitted. The permitted options include guardrails, nets, or PFAS, or a warning line combined with one of those systems or with a safety monitor.

- **A** is wrong: A monitor alone is limited to roofs 50 ft or less in width.
- **C** is wrong: A warning line is never a stand-alone system.
- **D** is wrong: Verbal reminders aren't a fall-protection system.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10)

**2. Answer: D** — *Safety monitor duties*

A safety monitor must be on the same walking/working surface, within visual sighting distance of the employees, and close enough to communicate orally. A monitor in the parking lot with a radio doesn't meet this.

- **A** is wrong: This is a requirement: no other responsibilities that could distract from monitoring.
- **B** is wrong: This is a requirement.
- **C** is wrong: This is a requirement.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(h)(1)

**3. Answer: A** — *Guardrail height*

Top rails must be 42 in plus or minus 3 in above the walking/working level, which is 39 to 45 in. 37 in is too low.

- **B** is wrong: 40 in is within the 39–45 in range.
- **C** is wrong: 42 in is the nominal height.
- **D** is wrong: 45 in is the upper limit and is compliant.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(b)(1)

**4. Answer: C** — *Guardrail strength*

Guardrail systems must withstand at least 200 lb applied within 2 in of the top edge in any outward or downward direction. Midrails must withstand 150 lb, and toeboards 50 lb.

- **A** is wrong: 50 lb is the toeboard requirement.
- **B** is wrong: 150 lb is the midrail requirement.
- **D** is wrong: 5,000 lb is the PFAS anchorage requirement.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(b)(3), (b)(5)

**5. Answer: B** — *Toeboards*

Toeboards must be at least 3½ in high (top edge to walking/working surface) and withstand at least 50 lb.

- **A** is wrong: 2 in is too low.
- **C** is wrong: 6 in exceeds the minimum.
- **D** is wrong: 12 in exceeds the minimum.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(j)(3)

**6. Answer: A** — *Covers*

Covers must support at least twice the weight of employees, equipment, and materials imposed. They must be secured so they can't be displaced, and marked with the word "HOLE" or "COVER" (or color-coded).

- **B** is wrong: A tarp is not a cover and hides the hazard.
- **C** is wrong: A loose, thin cover can shift or break.
- **D** is wrong: Paint and verbal warnings don't meet the strength and securing requirements.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(i)

**7. Answer: D** — *Safety nets*

Safety nets must be installed as close as practicable under the work, but never more than 30 ft below it.

- **A** is wrong: 6 ft is the general fall-protection trigger height.
- **B** is wrong: 15 ft is not the net rule.
- **C** is wrong: 25 ft is the maximum control-line distance from a leading edge, not the net distance.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(c)(1)

**8. Answer: B** — *Body belts*

Since January 1, 1998, body belts may not be used as part of a personal fall arrest system. They are allowed only in positioning systems.

- **A** is wrong: A shock-absorbing lanyard doesn't make a body belt acceptable for arrest.
- **C** is wrong: There is no low-slope exception.
- **D** is wrong: Free-fall distance doesn't create an exception.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)

**9. Answer: A** — *Snaphooks*

Only locking-type snaphooks, designed and used to prevent disengagement, are permitted. Non-locking snaphooks have been prohibited since 1998.

- **B** is wrong: Locking snaphooks are standard PFAS connectors.
- **C** is wrong: Daily inspection doesn't make a non-locking hook acceptable.
- **D** is wrong: A plain spring gate can roll out and disengage.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(5)

**10. Answer: B** — *Self-retracting lifelines*

Self-retracting lifelines and lanyards that automatically limit free fall to 2 ft or less must have a tensile strength of at least 3,000 lb. Those that don't must meet 5,000 lb.

- **A** is wrong: 1,800 lb is the maximum arresting force.
- **C** is wrong: 3,600 lb is the snaphook/D-ring proof test.
- **D** is wrong: 5,000 lb applies to ordinary lanyards and to SRLs that don't limit free fall to 2 ft.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(12)

**11. Answer: B** — *Fall clearance calculation*

Required clearance = 6 (free fall) + 3.5 (deceleration) + 1 (stretch) + 5 (D-ring to feet) + 2 (margin) = 17.5 ft. With only 14 ft, the worker could strike the ground. A self-retracting lifeline or different anchoring is needed.

- **A** is wrong: 12 ft leaves out the body length below the D-ring and the margin.
- **C** is wrong: 25 ft overstates the requirement.
- **D** is wrong: 9.5 ft counts only free fall and deceleration.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(16) (no contact with lower level; 6-ft free fall; 3.5-ft deceleration); ANSI Z359 clearance method

**12. Answer: A** — *Fall protection training records*

The certification record must include the name or other identity of the employee trained, the date(s) of training, and the signature of the person who conducted the training or of the employer.

- **B** is wrong: SSN and address aren't required elements.
- **C** is wrong: A license copy isn't required.
- **D** is wrong: The record must identify the employee and the date and be signed.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.503(b)

**13. Answer: D** — *Fall protection plan*

Fall protection plans are allowed only for leading-edge work, precast concrete erection, and residential construction, and only where conventional protection is infeasible or creates a greater hazard. The plan must be site-specific and prepared by a qualified person.

- **A** is wrong: Height alone doesn't qualify a job.
- **B** is wrong: Experience doesn't qualify a job.
- **C** is wrong: Commercial reroofing doesn't qualify, and owner agreement doesn't matter.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(13); 1926.502(k)

**14. Answer: C** — *Ladder extension above landing*

Portable ladders used to reach an upper landing must have side rails that extend at least 3 ft above it. Where that isn't possible, the ladder is secured at the top and a grasping device is provided.

- **A** is wrong: 1 ft is too short.
- **B** is wrong: 2 ft is too short.
- **D** is wrong: 5 ft is more than the minimum.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(1)

**15. Answer: A** — *Stepladder use*

The top or top step of a stepladder may not be used as a step. Stepladders must also be fully opened and locked, and used on stable, level surfaces.

- **B** is wrong: Nonconductive rails are the correct choice near electrical equipment.
- **C** is wrong: Stable, level footing is required.
- **D** is wrong: Fully opened with locked spreaders is the required setup.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(13)

**16. Answer: B** — *When stairways or ladders are required*

A stairway or ladder is required at all personnel points of access where there is a break in elevation of 19 in or more, and no ramp, runway, sloped embankment, or personnel hoist is provided.

- **A** is wrong: 12 in is below the threshold.
- **C** is wrong: 24 in is above the threshold.
- **D** is wrong: 36 in is above the threshold.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1051(a)

**17. Answer: C** — *Scaffold fall protection*

Under the scaffold standard, employees on a scaffold more than 10 ft above a lower level need fall protection.

- **A** is wrong: 4 ft is the general-industry threshold, not the construction scaffold rule.
- **B** is wrong: 6 ft is the Subpart M threshold for roofs and edges, not scaffolds.
- **D** is wrong: 15 ft is not the scaffold rule.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.451(g)(1)

**18. Answer: B** — *Scaffold inspection*

Scaffolds and their components must be inspected by a competent person before each work shift and after any occurrence that could affect structural integrity.

- **A** is wrong: A single inspection at erection is not enough.
- **C** is wrong: Storms are one trigger, but inspection is also required every shift.
- **D** is wrong: Weekly is not frequent enough.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.451(f)(3)

**19. Answer: A** — *Nail gun triggers*

A full sequential trigger fires only when the safety contact is pressed first and then the trigger is squeezed. It greatly reduces unintended discharges. Contact triggers have about twice the injury risk.

- **B** is wrong: Contact (bump) triggers are the highest-risk type.
- **C** is wrong: Single-shot contact triggers are still less protective than full sequential.
- **D** is wrong: Glasses protect the eyes but don't prevent unintended firing.

*Source:* OSHA/NIOSH "Nail Gun Safety: A Guide for Construction Contractors" (2011)

**20. Answer: A** — *Compressed air for cleaning*

Compressed air may be used for cleaning only where it is reduced to less than 30 psi, and then only with effective chip guarding and personal protective equipment.

- **B** is wrong: Gloves don't make high-pressure cleaning acceptable.
- **C** is wrong: It is permitted under the stated conditions.
- **D** is wrong: Higher pressure increases the hazard and isn't permitted.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.302(b)(4)

**21. Answer: B** — *PPE for hot asphalt*

Hot asphalt causes severe burns. Cover the skin completely with long sleeves and gauntlet gloves, and wear boots with the pant legs outside so hot material can't run into them. Protect the eyes and face with safety glasses and a face shield.

- **A** is wrong: Exposed skin and sneakers invite burns.
- **C** is wrong: A hard hat doesn't protect skin or eyes from splashes.
- **D** is wrong: Respiratory protection doesn't address burn hazards.

*Source:* NRCA safety practice (paywalled; not read); 29 CFR 1926.95, 1926.102

**22. Answer: B** — *Silica — cutting concrete tile*

For handheld power saws cutting silica-containing materials, Table 1 specifies integrated water delivery to the blade. Respirator requirements depend on the task's duration.

- **A** is wrong: Time of day doesn't control silica exposure.
- **C** is wrong: Standing upwind isn't a Table 1 engineering control.
- **D** is wrong: A dust mask without engineering controls isn't the Table 1 method and isn't an approved respirator.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1153(c) Table 1

**23. Answer: B** — *Asbestos PEL*

The asbestos PEL is 0.1 fiber per cubic centimeter as an 8-hour time-weighted average. The excursion limit is 1.0 f/cc averaged over 30 minutes.

- **A** is wrong: 0.01 f/cc is not the PEL.
- **C** is wrong: 1.0 f/cc is the 30-minute excursion limit.
- **D** is wrong: 5.0 f/cc is far above any current limit.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.1101(c)

**24. Answer: A** — *Fire extinguishers near flammable liquids*

At least one extinguisher rated not less than 10B must be within 50 ft of wherever more than 5 gallons of flammable or combustible liquids, or 5 lb of flammable gas, are being used on the jobsite.

- **B** is wrong: 100 ft is the travel-distance limit for the 2A building extinguishers, not this rule.
- **C** is wrong: The ground floor may be far more than 50 ft from the roof work.
- **D** is wrong: A truck may not be within 50 ft or accessible.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.150(c)(1)(iv)

**25. Answer: D** — *LP-gas storage*

OSHA prohibits storing LP-gas containers inside buildings. They must be stored outside, following the separation requirements for the quantity stored.

- **A** is wrong: Locking a room doesn't change the prohibition.
- **B** is wrong: A stairwell is inside a building and is a critical exit path.
- **C** is wrong: A mechanical room is inside the building.

*Source:* 29 CFR 1926.153(j)–(k)


## Section 2: Roofing Preparation & Materials

**26. Answer: B** — *Wood panel spacing*

A ⅛-in gap at panel ends and edges lets the panels expand with moisture without buckling. Tight panels can ridge, and the ridging telegraphs through the shingles.

- **A** is wrong: Tight joints cause buckling as the panels swell.
- **C** is wrong: ½ in is excessive and leaves unsupported shingle areas.
- **D** is wrong: 1 in is excessive.

*Source:* APA Engineered Wood Construction Guide; manufacturer panel stamps

**27. Answer: B** — *Panel edge clips*

Panel clips support the edges of adjacent panels between rafters or trusses, where the span rating calls for edge support. They reduce deflection at the joints and set the spacing.

- **A** is wrong: Underlayment is fastened separately.
- **C** is wrong: H-clips aren't lightning protection.
- **D** is wrong: Shingles are nailed to the deck, not clipped.

*Source:* APA roof sheathing guidance

**28. Answer: D** — *Wet deck during tear-off*

Covering a wet deck traps moisture. The underlayment wrinkles, the panels swell and buckle, and the shingles can blister or buckle. Dry the deck and replace damaged panels first.

- **A** is wrong: Covering a wet deck traps the water.
- **B** is wrong: Underlayment is required, and the deck is still wet.
- **C** is wrong: Coating traps moisture and isn't a steep-slope practice.

*Source:* NRCA steep-slope practice; manufacturer requirements (dry deck)

**29. Answer: C** — *Nuclear moisture survey*

A nuclear gauge emits fast neutrons that slow when they strike hydrogen atoms. The counts rise with hydrogen, mostly from water, though some insulations and asphalt add a baseline.

- **A** is wrong: Weight isn't measured.
- **B** is wrong: Capacitance is used by capacitance/impedance meters.
- **D** is wrong: Surface temperature is what infrared thermography measures.

*Source:* NRCA moisture survey practice

**30. Answer: C** — *Infrared survey timing*

Wet insulation stores more solar heat during the day and releases it slowly. After sunset, wet areas show up warmer than dry areas. Surface water and wind interfere with the reading.

- **A** is wrong: At noon the sun heats the whole surface and masks the differences.
- **B** is wrong: Surface water ruins the readings.
- **D** is wrong: Without solar loading, there is little heat difference to see.

*Source:* ASTM C1153; NRCA moisture survey practice

**31. Answer: A** — *Re-cover over tile*

The model codes prohibit installing new coverings over existing slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile. Tile isn't a suitable nail base, and the combined weight is a concern.

- **B** is wrong: Tile condition doesn't lift the prohibition.
- **C** is wrong: Nail length doesn't make the re-cover allowable.
- **D** is wrong: Underlayment doesn't make the re-cover allowable.

*Source:* IRC R908.3.1.1 / IBC 1511.3.1.1 [JURISDICTION]

**32. Answer: A** — *Self-adhered underlayment under metal*

Temperatures under metal roofing get very high. Standard-grade self-adhered membranes can soften, flow, and stick to the panels, which restricts their thermal movement. High-temperature grades are made for this.

- **B** is wrong: Architectural metal panels need underlayment.
- **C** is wrong: A standard grade may bleed or flow at metal-roof temperatures.
- **D** is wrong: Organic felt can stick to hot metal and doesn't provide self-adhered protection where it's needed.

*Source:* Underlayment and metal panel manufacturer instructions [MANUFACTURER]

**33. Answer: B** — *Synthetic underlayment exposure*

UV exposure limits vary by product, often from about 30 to 180 days. The manufacturer's limit governs. Past it, the underlayment may need replacing.

- **A** is wrong: No universal 12-month code rule exists.
- **C** is wrong: Synthetics degrade with UV.
- **D** is wrong: No universal 24-hour rule exists.

*Source:* ICC-ES AC188 evaluation reports; manufacturer data [MANUFACTURER]

**34. Answer: C** — *Coal tar compatibility*

Coal tar and asphalt are chemically incompatible, and mixing them can cause softening, poor adhesion, and failure. Repairs must use coal-tar-compatible materials or a manufacturer-approved method.

- **A** is wrong: EPDM is incompatible with bitumens unless it is separated.
- **B** is wrong: Bitumens aren't interchangeable.
- **D** is wrong: The concern is incompatibility and poor bonding, not simple hardening.

*Source:* NRCA BUR practice; ASTM D450

**35. Answer: A** — *Equiviscous temperature*

EVT is the temperature at which the asphalt has the right viscosity for mopping (or for mechanical spreading). Asphalt is applied at EVT ±25°F.

- **B** is wrong: The storage limit relates to the flash point and the finished blowing temperature (FBT).
- **C** is wrong: Ignition relates to the flash point.
- **D** is wrong: Softening relates to the softening point.

*Source:* NRCA BUR practice; asphalt bill of lading

**36. Answer: D** — *Overheating asphalt*

Prolonged overheating changes asphalt's properties. It can lower the softening point, making the finished membrane more prone to flow and slippage, and it increases fumes and fire risk.

- **A** is wrong: Overheating doesn't change compatibility with coal tar.
- **B** is wrong: Overheating degrades asphalt; it doesn't strengthen it.
- **C** is wrong: Quality is affected well below the flash point.

*Source:* NRCA BUR practice; asphalt manufacturer guidance

**37. Answer: D** — *Cover boards*

Cover boards (gypsum, high-density polyiso, perlite, fiberboard) protect the foam. They provide a smooth, strong substrate for adhesion and improve impact, traffic, and fire performance.

- **A** is wrong: They don't serve as vapor retarders unless specifically designed to.
- **B** is wrong: They add weight.
- **C** is wrong: Cover boards add little R-value.

*Source:* NRCA membrane practice; cover-board manufacturer data

**38. Answer: D** — *Night seals*

Night seals are temporary. If left in place, they can become dams that trap water and cause blisters or wet insulation under the new membrane.

- **A** is wrong: Drain holes defeat the seal and don't solve the buried-dam problem.
- **B** is wrong: Leaving it in creates a buried dam.
- **C** is wrong: Burying it causes the same problem.

*Source:* NRCA membrane practice

**39. Answer: B** — *Multiple insulation layers*

Joints through a single layer create continuous paths for heat loss and air (and moisture) movement. Offset joints in multiple layers block those paths.

- **A** is wrong: Codes don't generally prohibit single layers.
- **C** is wrong: Offsetting joints has nothing to do with slope.
- **D** is wrong: Multiple layers usually cost more to install.

*Source:* NRCA membrane/insulation practice

**40. Answer: B** — *Insulation thickness calculation*

Thickness = R ÷ (R per inch) = 30 ÷ 5.7 = 5.26 in, which rounds to about 5.3 in. In practice this is two layers, such as 2.6 in + 2.7 in, or the next available product thicknesses.

- **A** is wrong: 4.5 in gives only R-25.7.
- **C** is wrong: 5.7 in treats the R per inch as the thickness.
- **D** is wrong: 6.0 in exceeds the minimum.

*Source:* R-value arithmetic; manufacturer LTTR data

**41. Answer: C** — *Tapered insulation calculation*

Rise = 48 ft × 0.25 in/ft = 12 in. Add the ½-in starter: 12 + 0.5 = 12.5 in.

- **A** is wrong: 6.0 in uses ⅛ in per ft.
- **B** is wrong: 12.0 in forgets the ½-in starter thickness.
- **D** is wrong: 24.5 in uses ½ in per ft.

*Source:* Tapered insulation arithmetic

**42. Answer: D** — *Zinc and cedar*

Cedar leaches tannic acid when wet, and it corrodes zinc and galvanized coatings. Copper or stainless flashing is preferred with cedar.

- **A** is wrong: The flashing is under the shingles, out of the UV.
- **B** is wrong: Condensation isn't the typical cause in this pairing.
- **C** is wrong: Nail count doesn't cause coating corrosion.

*Source:* Cedar Shake & Shingle Bureau guidance; NRCA metal practice

**43. Answer: B** — *Roof area from plan*

1,800 × 1.083 = 1,949.4 sq ft = 19.49, or about 19.5 squares.

- **A** is wrong: 18.0 squares ignores the slope.
- **C** is wrong: 20.2 squares uses the 6:12 factor (1.118).
- **D** is wrong: 22.5 squares uses a factor of 1.25 (9:12).

*Source:* Slope factor = √(rise² + 144) ÷ 12

**44. Answer: B** — *Pitch vs slope*

Pitch = rise ÷ span. The span is twice the run, so pitch = 8 ÷ 24 = 1/3.

- **A** is wrong: 1/4 pitch corresponds to a 6:12 slope.
- **C** is wrong: 2/3 is the rise-to-run ratio (8:12), which is the slope, not the pitch.
- **D** is wrong: 8/12 is the slope expressed as a fraction, not the pitch.

*Source:* Roof geometry definitions (estimating texts)

**45. Answer: B** — *Felt rolls*

26 ÷ 4 = 6.5, which rounds up to 7 rolls.

- **A** is wrong: 6 rolls rounds down and leaves the roof short.
- **C** is wrong: 13 rolls uses the 2-squares-per-roll coverage of No. 30 felt.
- **D** is wrong: 26 rolls treats each roll as covering one square.

*Source:* Standard roll coverage (manufacturer data)


## Section 3: Steep-Slope Roofing Systems

**46. Answer: D** — *Shingle wind rating*

ASTM D7158 classes are D (about 90 mph), G (about 120 mph), and H (about 150 mph). Class H is the highest.

- **A** is wrong: Class D is the lowest D7158 class.
- **B** is wrong: Class F belongs to ASTM D3161, a different test standard.
- **C** is wrong: Class G is in the middle.

*Source:* ASTM D7158; ASTM D3161

**47. Answer: D** — *Impact rating*

UL 2218 rates impact resistance from Class 1 to Class 4. Class 4 is the most resistant, and some insurers offer discounts for it.

- **A** is wrong: Class 1 is the lowest.
- **B** is wrong: Class 2 is lower than Class 4.
- **C** is wrong: Class 3 is lower than Class 4.

*Source:* UL 2218

**48. Answer: A** — *Fire classification*

Class A indicates effectiveness against severe fire exposure. Class B is moderate and Class C is light. Most asphalt shingles are Class A.

- **B** is wrong: Class B is lower.
- **C** is wrong: Class C is the lowest classified level.
- **D** is wrong: E108 has no Class D.

*Source:* ASTM E108 / UL 790

**49. Answer: D** — *Underlayment on 4:12 and steeper*

For 4:12 and steeper, a single layer is applied parallel to the eave, shingle-fashion, with 2-in head laps and staggered end laps, per the code and the manufacturer.

- **A** is wrong: Underlayment is required.
- **B** is wrong: Two layers with 19-in laps is the method for 2:12 to below 4:12.
- **C** is wrong: Vertical application creates laps that run water into the seams.

*Source:* IRC R905.1.1 [JURISDICTION]; manufacturer instructions

**50. Answer: A** — *Starter strip error*

A starter's purpose is to put sealant at the eave to bond the first course and to back the first course's cutouts. Flipping a 3-tab moves the sealant away from the eave edge. Use a purpose-made starter, or a 3-tab with the tabs removed.

- **B** is wrong: Manufacturers call for starters with the sealant near the eave edge.
- **C** is wrong: The fire rating isn't the issue.
- **D** is wrong: Weight isn't the issue.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions

**51. Answer: D** — *Racking installation*

Many manufacturers discourage or prohibit racking. It can produce visible shading and banding and alter offset and fastener patterns. Diagonal application is recommended.

- **A** is wrong: Racking doesn't affect ventilation.
- **B** is wrong: Racking doesn't affect sealing.
- **C** is wrong: Racking doesn't improve wind resistance.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions [MANUFACTURER]

**52. Answer: A** — *Production lots*

Color can vary between production runs. Using bundles from the same lot, and pulling from several bundles as you go, avoids visible patches.

- **B** is wrong: Ventilation doesn't create color patches.
- **C** is wrong: Nail length doesn't affect color.
- **D** is wrong: Underlayment doesn't affect the visible color.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions

**53. Answer: C** — *Nail penetration*

Nails must penetrate at least ¾ in into the deck or through the deck, whichever is less. With ⅜-in sheathing, the nail must go completely through it.

- **A** is wrong: ½ in doesn't meet the rule.
- **B** is wrong: ¾-in penetration isn't possible in a ⅜-in deck. The rule is ¾ in or through.
- **D** is wrong: The nails must engage the deck.

*Source:* IRC R905.2.5 [JURISDICTION]; manufacturer instructions

**54. Answer: A** — *Overdriven nails*

Overdriven nails break through the shingle and lose their ability to hold it. Nails should be driven flush with the shingle surface.

- **B** is wrong: Rust isn't the concern here.
- **C** is wrong: Nails must be flush, not driven deep.
- **D** is wrong: Overdriving doesn't speed sealing.

*Source:* Manufacturer installation instructions

**55. Answer: A** — *Staples*

Staples are easily misapplied (crooked, over- or under-driven) and hold less reliably. NRCA doesn't recommend them, and many manufacturers restrict warranty coverage when they are used.

- **B** is wrong: High-wind areas call for more nails, not staples.
- **C** is wrong: Nails are the preferred fastener.
- **D** is wrong: Steep slopes call for nails plus hand-sealing.

*Source:* NRCA practice; manufacturer warranty terms [MANUFACTURER]

**56. Answer: B** — *Curling and cupping*

Heat and moisture building up under the deck speed shingle aging and distortion. The ventilated garage, with the same shingles, shows that the attic conditions are the difference.

- **A** is wrong: Underlayment doesn't cause curling.
- **C** is wrong: Starter strips don't cause curling.
- **D** is wrong: Drip edge doesn't cause curling.

*Source:* Shingle manufacturer ventilation requirements; NRCA ventilation practice

**57. Answer: D** — *Buckling shingles*

When wet felt or sheathing is covered, the underlayment wrinkles and the panels move as they dry. The distortion shows through the shingles as buckling.

- **A** is wrong: High nailing causes blow-offs, not waves.
- **B** is wrong: Algae causes dark streaks.
- **C** is wrong: Ventilation deficiencies cause long-term aging, not immediate buckling after a wet install.

*Source:* NRCA steep-slope practice; manufacturer instructions

**58. Answer: D** — *Algae streaks*

Dark streaks are typically algae (*Gloeocapsa magma*), a cosmetic problem. Algae-resistant granules, or copper and zinc strips that release inhibiting ions in runoff, help prevent it.

- **A** is wrong: A cricket doesn't address algae.
- **B** is wrong: Streaks aren't asphalt bleeding.
- **C** is wrong: Surface algae isn't deck rot.

*Source:* Shingle manufacturer technical bulletins

**59. Answer: C** — *Open valley width*

Widening the open portion toward the eave handles the increasing water volume and lets ice and debris pass freely. The shingle edges are trimmed along chalk lines.

- **A** is wrong: Narrowing toward the eave concentrates the water where the flow is greatest.
- **B** is wrong: A constant width is sometimes used, but the common recommendation is to widen it.
- **D** is wrong: A zero-width eave closes the valley.

*Source:* Manufacturer and NRCA valley practice [MANUFACTURER]

**60. Answer: C** — *Roll-roofing valley lining*

The code method for mineral-surfaced roll roofing valleys is two layers: an 18-in bottom layer placed granules down, then a 36-in top layer granules up.

- **A** is wrong: Roll roofing is a listed valley lining.
- **B** is wrong: A single 12-in strip is too narrow and lacks the second layer.
- **D** is wrong: The layer widths and orientation are reversed.

*Source:* IRC Table R905.2.8.2 [JURISDICTION]

**61. Answer: B** — *Valley underlayment sequence*

The valley lining goes on first, centered in the valley. The field underlayment from each plane laps over it, so water in the field flows over the valley layer, not under it.

- **A** is wrong: Field underlayment first would place the valley strip's edges where water can run under them.
- **C** is wrong: Underlayment always goes under the shingles.
- **D** is wrong: Valleys carry the most water and need underlayment.

*Source:* NRCA and manufacturer valley practice

**62. Answer: C** — *Step flashing size*

Each step flashing piece should be about 2 in longer than the exposure, so each piece laps the one below by about 2 in. For a 5-in exposure, that's about 7 in. The code minimum leg dimension is 4 × 4 in.

Retrofit framing and new panels add weight and change load paths. The structure and the attachment must be evaluated for dead, snow, and wind loads.

- **A** is wrong: Retrofit systems are designed to go over the existing panels.
- **B** is wrong: Color is irrelevant.
- **C** is wrong: Structural evaluation is essential.

*Source:* Metal building and retrofit manufacturer guidance; building code [JURISDICTION]

**123. Answer: D** — *SPF chemistry*

SPF is made by mixing isocyanate (A side) with a polyol resin blend (B side) at the spray gun. Isocyanates are respiratory sensitizers, so they require respiratory protection and exposure controls.

- **A** is wrong: Primer and granules aren't the two reactive components.
- **B** is wrong: The coating is a separate protective layer applied afterward.
- **C** is wrong: SPF isn't an asphalt system.

*Source:* SPF industry guidance; NRCA SPF manual (paywalled)

**124. Answer: D** — *SPF UV protection*

UV breaks down exposed polyurethane foam. An elastomeric coating (acrylic, silicone, urethane) or aggregate protects it, and it must be applied within the manufacturer's time window.

- **A** is wrong: Compatibility isn't the reason.
- **B** is wrong: The foam provides the R-value.
- **C** is wrong: Weight isn't the reason.

*Source:* SPF manufacturer guidance

**125. Answer: C** — *SPF overspray*

SPF overspray can travel long distances and damage cars, windows, and equipment. Proactive controls include wind monitoring, screens, masking, vehicle relocation, and stopping work in high winds.

- **A** is wrong: Rushing increases overspray risk and affects quality.
- **B** is wrong: Higher pressure doesn't prevent drift and can make it worse.
- **D** is wrong: After-the-fact notice doesn't prevent the damage.

*Source:* SPF industry and manufacturer guidance

**126. Answer: B** — *Coating over wet insulation*

Coating over wet insulation traps moisture. That leads to blistering, loss of R-value, deck corrosion, and coating failure. Wet areas must be replaced first.

- **A** is wrong: Extra thickness doesn't remove water.
- **C** is wrong: Vent holes don't dry saturated insulation and create leak paths.
- **D** is wrong: Sealing moisture in causes failures.

*Source:* Roof coating manufacturer and NRCA restoration practice

**127. Answer: A** — *Vegetative roof leak testing*

Finding and fixing leaks is far easier before the overburden goes on. Flood testing or electronic leak detection is done on the exposed membrane.

- **B** is wrong: Waiting for a leak means removing overburden to find it.
- **C** is wrong: Established plants make access costly.
- **D** is wrong: Flood tests and electronic methods are standard.

*Source:* NRCA vegetative roof guidance; ASTM D5957 / D7877 practice

**128. Answer: A** — *Pavers and structure*

Pavers weigh more than typical stone ballast, and an occupied terrace adds live load. A structural evaluation is essential.

- **B** is wrong: The fire rating is secondary to structural capacity.
- **C** is wrong: Color is secondary.
- **D** is wrong: Pavers add little R-value.

*Source:* Paver/PMR manufacturer guidance; building code live loads [JURISDICTION]

**129. Answer: A** — *Positive-side waterproofing*

Waterproofing membranes are normally applied on the positive side, where the water pressure comes from. The water then pushes the membrane against the structure instead of away from it.

- **B** is wrong: The membrane isn't installed in the wall cavity.
- **C** is wrong: Performance differs greatly between the two sides.
- **D** is wrong: Negative-side systems are limited (for example, cementitious crystalline products); sheet membranes aren't normally installed there.

*Source:* NRCA Waterproofing Manual (2005 — paywalled; not read); industry practice

**130. Answer: C** — *NDL warranties*

NDL warranties remove the dollar cap for covered repairs. They carry conditions (approved applicators, inspections, maintenance) and exclusions (for example, abuse, alterations, and some weather events beyond the stated limits).

- **A** is wrong: Workmanship warranties come from the contractor and are separate.
- **B** is wrong: Inspections and approved-installer status are typical requirements.
- **D** is wrong: Warranties have exclusions.

*Source:* Manufacturer warranty terms [MANUFACTURER]; NRCA warranty guidance
