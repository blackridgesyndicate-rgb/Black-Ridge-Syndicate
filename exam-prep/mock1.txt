## Mock Exam 1 source file. Each item begins with a Q-marker line; the build script may reorder options to balance the key (except KEEP items).

### Q
S: 1
T: Fall protection — low-slope roof, narrow building
Q: A three-person crew is tearing off and replacing a built-up roof on a flat (¼:12) roof that is 42 ft wide and 120 ft long. The edges are 28 ft above grade. No mechanical equipment will be used. Which fall-protection approach is permitted by itself, without any other system?
A: A safety monitoring system with a competent-person monitor
B: A warning line system set 6 ft from all edges
C: Toeboards installed along every roof edge
D: Having workers stay at least 10 ft from the edge whenever possible
ANS: A
EXP: For roofing work on low-slope roofs, 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10) allows a safety monitoring system alone when the roof is 50 ft or less in width. This roof is 42 ft wide, so a monitor alone is permitted.
WB: A warning line only marks an area. It must be combined with guardrails, nets, PFAS, or a safety monitor, because work still happens between the line and the edge.
WC: Toeboards protect people below from falling objects. They do not stop a worker from falling.
WD: "Stay back when possible" is not a fall-protection system recognized by OSHA.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10); 1926.502(h)

### Q
S: 1
T: Fall protection — steep residential roof
Q: A crew is reroofing a two-story house with a 7:12 slope. The eaves are 19 ft above grade. Which is the BEST fall protection for this job?
A: Personal fall arrest systems attached to roof anchors fastened into the framing per the anchor manufacturer's instructions
B: A warning line along the eaves combined with a safety monitor
C: A safety monitor who watches all workers from the ground
D: Roof brackets with a 2×6 slide guard at the eave, and no other protection
ANS: A
EXP: A 7:12 roof is a steep roof (greater than 4:12). Steep roofs require guardrails with toeboards, safety nets, or PFAS (1926.501(b)(11)). For residential construction (1926.501(b)(13)), conventional protection is presumed feasible. PFAS on properly installed anchors is the standard, compliant choice.
WB: Warning lines and safety monitors are only permitted for roofing work on low-slope roofs (4:12 or less).
WC: A monitor must be on the same walking/working surface as the workers, and monitors aren't permitted on steep roofs anyway.
WD: Slide guards alone are not an OSHA fall-protection system. The old interim residential guidance that allowed them was rescinded.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(11), (b)(13); OSHA STD 03-11-002 (2010)

### Q
S: 1
T: Warning line — mechanical equipment
Q: A mechanical felt-laying machine will travel east–west on a low-slope roof. How far from the east and west roof edges, which are perpendicular to the machine's direction of travel, must the warning line be at minimum?
KEEP
A: 6 ft
B: 8 ft
C: 10 ft
D: 15 ft
ANS: C
EXP: When mechanical equipment is used, the warning line must be at least 6 ft from edges parallel to the direction of travel and at least 10 ft from edges perpendicular to it. The machine could run straight toward those edges.
WA: 6 ft is the distance for edges parallel to travel, or for work with no mechanical equipment.
WB: 8 ft is not a value in the warning-line rule.
WD: 15 ft exceeds the minimum. It is allowed, but it is not the required minimum.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.502(f)(1)(ii)

### Q
S: 1
T: Warning line construction
Q: A foreman inspects a newly erected warning line. Which condition violates OSHA's warning-line requirements?
A: The rope sags to 31 in above the roof surface between two stanchions
B: The line is flagged with high-visibility material every 5 ft
C: The line's highest point is 38 in above the roof surface
D: The stanchions resist a 20-lb horizontal push applied 30 in above the roof without tipping
ANS: A
EXP: The line's lowest point, including sag, must be no less than 34 in above the walking/working surface, and its highest point no more than 39 in. A 31-in sag is too low.
WB: Flagging is required at intervals of not more than 6 ft, so 5 ft complies.
WC: 38 in is within the 34–39 in range.
WD: Stanchions must resist at least 16 lb at 30 in, so 20 lb exceeds the requirement.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.502(f)(2)

### Q
S: 1
T: PFAS — anchorage location and free fall
Q: A roofer wears a full-body harness with a 6-ft shock-absorbing lanyard. The lanyard is clipped to a roof anchor at the level of the roofer's feet on a 6:12 roof. What is the MAIN problem with this setup?
A: The potential free fall is about 11 ft, which exceeds OSHA's 6-ft free-fall limit
B: Lanyards must never be used on steep roofs
C: The anchor must be rated for 1,800 lb
D: There is no problem, because the shock absorber reduces the arresting force
ANS: A
EXP: With the anchor at foot level, the worker falls the lanyard length plus the height of the back D-ring (about 5 ft) before the lanyard even starts to arrest: roughly 11 ft. A PFAS must be rigged so free fall is no more than 6 ft and the worker cannot contact a lower level. Fixes include an overhead or ridge anchor with a shorter connection, or a self-retracting lifeline or lanyard rated for foot-level anchorage.
WB: Lanyards are allowed on steep roofs when the PFAS is rigged correctly.
WC: 1,800 lb is the maximum arresting force on the worker, not the anchorage strength. Anchorage is 5,000 lb per worker, or designed with a safety factor of 2 by a qualified person.
WD: A shock absorber limits the arresting force, but it does not cure excess free fall or the risk of hitting a lower level.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(16)(iii); (d)(15)

### Q
S: 1
T: PFAS after a fall
Q: A roofer slips, and his PFAS stops the fall. He is uninjured and is quickly helped back onto the roof. What should happen to his harness and lanyard?
A: Remove them from service until a competent person inspects them and determines they are undamaged
B: The worker may put them back on if he sees no visible damage
C: Replace only the lanyard; harnesses are not affected by a fall
D: Continue using them for the rest of the shift, then send them for inspection
ANS: A
EXP: Components subjected to impact loading must be removed from service immediately. They may not be used again until a competent person inspects them and determines they are undamaged and suitable for reuse. Many manufacturers require destroying equipment that arrested a fall.
WB: The worker is not necessarily a competent person, and internal damage may not be visible.
WC: Harness webbing and stitching can also be damaged in an arrest.
WD: Continued use after an arrest is exactly what the rule prohibits.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(19)

### Q
S: 1
T: PFAS anchorage strength
Q: A PFAS anchorage is not designed by a qualified person. What minimum load must it support per employee attached?
KEEP
A: 1,800 lb
B: 3,000 lb
C: 3,600 lb
D: 5,000 lb
ANS: D
EXP: Anchorages for PFAS must support at least 5,000 lb per employee attached. The alternative is an anchorage designed, installed, and used as part of a complete PFAS with a safety factor of at least two, under the supervision of a qualified person.
WA: 1,800 lb is the maximum arresting force allowed on the body with a harness.
WB: 3,000 lb applies to self-retracting lifelines that limit free fall to 2 ft or less, and to positioning-device anchorages.
WC: 3,600 lb is the proof-test load for snaphooks and D-rings.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.502(d)(15)

### Q
S: 1
T: Skylights as holes
Q: Crew members will work near several plastic dome skylights while replacing a low-slope roof. How should the skylights be protected?
A: With covers or screens able to support at least twice the imposed load, or with guardrails around them, or by PFAS
B: The domes are adequate if they are polycarbonate rather than acrylic
C: With a warning line set 6 ft from each skylight
D: By painting the curbs a high-visibility color
ANS: A
EXP: Skylights are treated as holes. Employees must be protected from falling through them by covers, guardrails, or PFAS. Covers must support at least twice the weight of the employees, equipment, and materials that may be imposed, and must be secured and marked.
WB: A skylight dome is not a cover, and falls through domes are a leading cause of roofing deaths.
WC: Warning lines are not a permitted protection for holes.
WD: Marking a curb is a warning, not fall protection.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(4); 1926.502(i)

### Q
S: 1
T: Ladder setup calculation
Q: An extension ladder leans against a gutter line 20 ft above level ground. Using OSHA's 4-to-1 rule, how far from the wall should the base of the ladder be?
KEEP
A: 3 ft
B: 4 ft
C: 5 ft
D: 6 ft 8 in
ANS: C
EXP: The horizontal distance from the top support to the foot should be about one-quarter of the working length. That is commonly figured as one-quarter of the height to the support point: 20 ft ÷ 4 = 5 ft. The ladder rails must also extend 3 ft above the landing.
WA: 3 ft is too steep, and the ladder could tip backward.
WB: 4 ft would be correct for a 16-ft support height.
WD: 6 ft 8 in is a 3-to-1 ratio. That is too shallow, and the base could kick out.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(5)(i), (b)(1)

### Q
S: 1
T: Ladders near power
Q: A worker must set a ladder where it could contact the service drop to the house. Which ladder choice is correct?
A: A ladder with nonconductive (e.g., fiberglass) side rails, set and used so that clearance from the line is maintained
B: A Type IA aluminum ladder, if it is tied off at the top
C: A wooden ladder, which is always safe near energized lines
D: An aluminum ladder with rubber feet, which insulate it from ground
ANS: A

WD: Rubber feet do not insulate the rails. Current flows through the worker.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(12); 1926.416

C: 10 ft

### Q
S: 1
T: Noise dose calculation
Q: During a tear-off, a worker is exposed to 95 dBA for 4 hours and 100 dBA for 2 hours. Using OSHA's permissible times (95 dBA = 4 h, 100 dBA = 2 h), what is the noise dose, and is the exposure acceptable?
KEEP
A: 0.75; within the limit
B: 1.00; exactly at the limit
C: 1.50; over the limit
D: 2.00; over the limit
ANS: D
EXP: Dose = C1/T1 + C2/T2 = 4/4 + 2/2 = 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0. Any sum greater than 1.0 exceeds the permissible exposure, so controls and hearing protection are required.
WA: 0.75 comes from using the wrong permissible times.
WB: 1.00 counts only one of the two exposures.
WC: 1.50 is an arithmetic error; each fraction equals 1.0.
SRC: 29 CFR 1926.52(d) and Table D-2

T: Asbestos — before tear-off
Q: A 1970s built-up roof is to be torn off. Its black flashing cement and old felts are suspected of containing asbestos. What is the BEST first step?
A: Have the suspect materials sampled and analyzed before disturbing them

WD: Re-covering is limited by code and doesn't remove the need to identify ACM that will be disturbed during the work.

### Q
S: 1
T: Kettle fire
Q: The asphalt in a kettle ignites. What is the BEST immediate action?
A: Close the kettle lid, shut off the burner, and use an appropriate extinguisher if needed
B: Spray water into the kettle to cool the asphalt
C: Stir the asphalt so the burning surface is submerged
D: Add cold asphalt to lower the temperature quickly
ANS: A
EXP: Closing the lid smothers the fire by cutting off oxygen. Shutting off the fuel removes the heat source. A B-rated extinguisher can handle any remaining flame.
WB: Water on hot asphalt flashes to steam and sprays burning asphalt, making the fire far worse.
WC: Stirring feeds oxygen and spreads the fire.
WD: Adding material near an open fire exposes the worker and does not stop combustion.
SRC: NRCA safety practice (NRCA Safety Manual — paywalled; not read); 29 CFR 1926.150

### Q
S: 1
T: Heating asphalt
Q: Which temperature must hot roofing asphalt NEVER reach in the kettle?
A: Its flash point
B: Its equiviscous temperature (EVT)
C: Its softening point
D: 350°F
ANS: A
EXP: Asphalt must never be heated to its flash point, where its vapors can ignite. Common practice keeps the kettle well below the flash point (often at least 25°F below). Application is done at the EVT ±25°F.
WB: EVT is the target application temperature, so it must be reached.
WC: The softening point (roughly 135–225°F) is far below application temperature and is necessarily exceeded.
WD: A fixed 350°F isn't the rule. Many asphalts are applied near or above 400°F, depending on their EVT.
SRC: NRCA practice; asphalt bill of lading/SDS (flash point, EVT, FBT)

### Q
S: 1
T: Heat illness
Q: On a hot afternoon a roofer becomes confused, stops sweating, and has hot, dry skin. What is the most likely condition, and the correct response?
A: Heat stroke — call 911 and begin aggressive cooling immediately
B: Heat cramps — have the worker stretch and drink an electrolyte drink
C: Heat exhaustion — let the worker rest in the truck until the shift ends
D: Mild dehydration — give water and return him to work in 15 minutes
ANS: A
EXP: Confusion and hot, dry skin with little sweating are signs of heat stroke, a medical emergency. Call 911 and cool the worker aggressively (shade, ice or cold water, fanning) while waiting.
WB: Heat cramps involve painful muscle spasms without confusion.
WC: Heat exhaustion typically shows heavy sweating and cool, clammy skin. Leaving a confused worker alone in a hot truck is dangerous.
WD: Returning a confused worker to the roof ignores a life-threatening emergency.
SRC: OSHA/NIOSH heat illness guidance; NRCA safety practice

### Q

T: Moisture survey confirmation
Q: An infrared scan of a low-slope roof shows several warm areas after sunset. What is the BEST way to confirm whether the insulation in those areas is wet?
A: Take core cuts at the suspect areas and at a known-dry reference area

### Q
S: 2
T: Galvanic corrosion
Q: A copper counterflashing drains onto a galvanized steel gutter. What is the most likely long-term result?
A: Accelerated corrosion of the galvanized gutter
B: Accelerated corrosion of the copper counterflashing
C: No reaction, because both metals are corrosion-resistant
D: Green staining on the gutter, with no loss of metal
