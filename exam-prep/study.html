1.1 Fall protection: the framework (the most-tested safety topic)

Plain English

Anyone working 6 ft or more above a lower level on a construction site needs fall protection. What counts as acceptable depends on the roof slope, the type of work, and whether it's residential construction.

Terms you must recognize (29 CFR 1926.500(b) definitions) [OSHA]

  • Low-slope roof: slope ≤ 4 in 12 (vertical to horizontal).
  • Steep roof: slope > 4 in 12.
  • Roofing work: hoisting, storage, application, and removal of roofing materials and equipment, including related insulation, sheet metal, and vapor-barrier work. It does not include constructing the roof deck.
  • Unprotected sides and edges: any side or edge where there is no wall or guardrail at least 39 in high.
  • Hole: a gap or void 2 in or more in its least dimension.
  • Competent person (1926.32(f)): capable of identifying hazards and has authority to take prompt corrective measures.
  • Qualified person (1926.32(m)): has a recognized degree, certificate, or professional standing, or extensive knowledge, training, and experience, and can solve problems relating to the subject.

Which paragraph applies? (1926.501(b)) [OSHA]

Situation Paragraph Permitted protection
Any unprotected side/edge ≥ 6 ft (b)(1) Guardrail, safety net, or personal fall arrest system (PFAS)
Holes, including skylights (b)(4) Covers, guardrails, or PFAS. Skylights count as holes.
Roofing work on low-slope roofs with edges ≥ 6 ft above lower level (b)(10) Guardrail, safety net, or PFAS, or a combination of: warning line + guardrail; warning line + safety net; warning line + PFAS; warning line + safety monitoring. On roofs 50 ft or less in width, safety monitoring alone is permitted.
Steep roofs with edges ≥ 6 ft (b)(11) Guardrail with toeboards, safety net, or PFAS. No warning lines, no safety monitor.
Residential construction ≥ 6 ft (b)(13) Guardrail, safety net, or PFAS. If the employer can show these are infeasible or create a greater hazard, a written fall protection plan per 1926.502(k) may be used.

What the roofer sees and what the question tests: a 3-story flat roof, 40 ft wide, reroof crew → (b)(10), and monitor-only is legal because width ≤ 50 ft. A 6:12 house roof → (b)(11) or (b)(13) → PFAS is the typical answer. "Slide guards/roof brackets alone" are not conventional fall protection. OSHA's old interim residential guidance that allowed slide guards was rescinded (STD 03-11-002, 2010). [OSHA]

Common wrong answers and why they tempt

Wrong answer Why it's tempting Why it's wrong
"Warning line alone" on a low-slope roof Warning lines are roof-specific A warning line only marks an area; outside it (and at the edge) you need another system. It is never a stand-alone system
"Safety monitor alone" on a 75-ft-wide low-slope roof Monitors are allowed on low-slope roofs Alone only if width ≤ 50 ft. Otherwise it must be combined with a warning line
"Safety monitor" on a 6:12 roof Workers are close to the edge (b)(11) permits only guardrail + toeboards, net, or PFAS
"Fall protection starts at 10 ft" That's the scaffold threshold (1926.451(g)) Roofs/edges: 6 ft
"Low slope means under 3:12" Industry sometimes calls ≤3:12 low slope OSHA's line is 4:12. 4:12 itself is low-slope (≤)

1.2 Warning lines, safety monitors, guardrails, nets, covers [OSHA-V for 1926.502]

Warning line systems (1926.502(f))

Requirement Value
Distance from roof edge, no mechanical equipment ≥ 6 ft
With mechanical equipment ≥ 6 ft from edges parallel to direction of equipment travel; ≥ 10 ft from edges perpendicular to it
Height of line (lowest point incl. sag / highest point) 34 in min / 39 in max
Flagging High-visibility material at ≤ 6-ft intervals
Stanchion strength Must resist 16 lb applied horizontally, 30 in above surface, toward the edge, without tipping
Rope/wire/chain tensile strength ≥ 500 lb
Access paths Access, materials handling, storage, and hoisting areas connect to the work area by a path formed by two warning lines. When the path is not in use, close it with a rope/wire/chain equal in strength and height, or offset it so no one can walk straight into the work area
No work between line and edge Unless protected by another system (guardrail, net, PFAS, or safety monitor)
Mechanical equipment (1926.502(f)(3)) Used or stored only where employees are protected by warning line, guardrail, or PFAS. Not in a monitor-only area

Safety monitoring systems (1926.502(h))

The monitor must be a competent person who:

  • recognizes fall hazards and warns employees when they are unaware of a hazard or acting unsafely;
  • is on the same walking/working surface and within visual sighting distance;
  • is close enough to communicate orally;
  • has no other responsibilities that could take the monitor's attention away.

Other rules:

  • No mechanical equipment is used or stored in monitored areas on low-slope roofs.
  • Only employees doing roofing work (or covered by a fall protection plan) may be in a monitored area.
  • Employees must comply promptly with the monitor's warnings.

Guardrail systems (1926.502(b))

Item Value
Top rail height 42 in ± 3 in (39–45 in)
Midrail Midway between top rail and surface (for a 42-in top rail, ≈ 21 in)
Top rail strength 200 lb outward/downward within 2 in of top edge; under that load it may not deflect below 39 in
Midrail strength 150 lb
Toeboard 3½ in high min; 50 lb strength; required where objects could fall. Required on steep roofs in (b)(11)
Wire rope top rail Flagged at ≤ 6 ft; ≥ ¼ in diameter
At hoisting areas Chain, gate, or removable section across the access opening when hoisting is not taking place

Safety nets (1926.502(c))

  • As close as practicable under the work surface, and never more than 30 ft below it.
  • Horizontal extension beyond the edge: 8 ft (net ≤ 5 ft below), 10 ft (5–10 ft below), 13 ft (> 10 ft below).
  • Drop test: 400-lb bag of sand, 30 ± 2 in diameter.
  • Mesh openings: ≤ 36 sq in and ≤ 6 in on a side.

Covers (1926.502(i))

  • Support at least twice the weight of employees, equipment, and materials that may be imposed.
  • Secured against displacement.
  • Marked "HOLE" or "COVER" (or color-coded).
  • Vehicle-traffic covers: twice the axle load.
  • Skylights: screens or covers rated for the load, or guardrails, or PFAS. A plastic skylight dome is NOT a cover.

Falling-object protection and materials near edges (1926.502(j)) [OSHA]

  • During roofing work, materials and equipment must not be stored within 6 ft of a roof edge unless guardrails are erected at the edge.
  • Materials piled near a roof edge must be stable and self-supporting.
  • (Contrast: the 4-ft storage rule in 1926.502(j) is for overhand bricklaying/masonry, a common distractor.)

1.3 Personal fall arrest systems (PFAS) (1926.502(d)) [OSHA]

Plain English

A harness, a connector (lanyard, self-retracting lifeline, or rope grab on a lifeline), and an anchor. It stops a fall that has already started. Positioning and restraint are different: they keep you from falling (or limit it to 2 ft).

Numbers to memorize

Component / criterion Requirement
Anchorage 5,000 lb per employee attached, OR designed, installed, and used as part of a complete PFAS with a safety factor of at least 2, under supervision of a qualified person
Connectors (D-rings, snaphooks) Min tensile 5,000 lb; proof-tested to 3,600 lb
Snaphooks Locking type only (non-locking prohibited since 1998)
Lanyards and vertical lifelines Min breaking strength 5,000 lb
Self-retracting lifelines/lanyards that limit free fall to ≤ 2 ft 3,000 lb min; otherwise 5,000 lb
Vertical lifelines One employee per lifeline
Max arresting force (with body harness) 1,800 lb
Max deceleration distance 3.5 ft
Max free fall 6 ft, and the worker must not contact any lower level
Body belts Not permitted as part of a PFAS (since Jan 1, 1998). Allowed only for positioning
After a fall Remove from service until inspected by a competent person and found undamaged
Rescue Employer must provide for prompt rescue or assure self-rescue
Inspection Before each use, for wear, damage, and deterioration
Attachment points Not to guardrails or hoists unless they meet anchorage criteria
Positioning device systems (1926.502(e)) Free fall ≤ 2 ft; anchorage ≥ 2× potential impact load or 3,000 lb, whichever is greater

What the roofer sees / decision tested

  • A roof anchor nailed through the sheathing only (not into a rafter/truss) → inadequate anchorage. Manufacturer instructions typically require fastening into framing [MANUFACTURER].
  • A 6-ft shock-absorbing lanyard clipped to an anchor at the worker's feet → free fall ≈ 6 ft + D-ring height (≈ 5 ft) ≈ 11 ft > 6-ft limit. Correct fix: rig the anchor overhead, shorten the lanyard, or use an SRL/lanyard rated for foot-level anchorage. The best answer depends on the options, but the flaw is the free-fall distance.
  • A worker fell and was arrested with no injury. Best next step: rescue promptly, then remove the harness and lanyard from service until a competent person inspects them. Do not simply reuse them.

Worked calculation: required clearance below the anchor

This is an industry method (ANSI/ASSP Z359 and manufacturer charts), not an OSHA formula. OSHA sets only the 6-ft free fall, 3.5-ft deceleration, and no-contact rules.

Worker with a 6-ft shock-absorbing lanyard, anchor at the D-ring level (≈ 5 ft above feet):

Step Value
Free fall (lanyard length, anchor at D-ring height) 6.0 ft
Deceleration distance (OSHA max) 3.5 ft
Harness stretch / D-ring shift (industry allowance) 1.0 ft
D-ring to bottom of feet 5.0 ft
Safety margin (industry) 2.0 ft
Total clearance needed below anchor 17.5 ft

If the lower level is only 14 ft below the anchor, this system would let the worker strike the ground. Use an SRL, which reduces free fall to ≤ 2 ft (verify with the manufacturer's clearance chart).

Common wrong answers

  • "Anchorage 3,000 lb." That is the SRL (≤ 2 ft free fall) and positioning figure, not the general anchorage.
  • "Free fall 4 ft." No; the limit is 6 ft.
  • "Body belt is fine for arrest." Prohibited since 1998.
  • "Two workers can share one vertical lifeline." Prohibited.

WHAT TO MEMORIZE 6 ft trigger · 4:12 line (≤ low, > steep) · 50-ft width monitor-only · warning line 6 ft / 10 ft (equipment perpendicular) · 34–39 in line height · flags every 6 ft · 16 lb @ 30 in · 500 lb line Guardrail 42 ± 3 in, 200 lb / 150 lb midrail, toeboard 3½ in / 50 lb Anchorage 5,000 lb per person (or SF 2 by qualified person) · 1,800 lb arrest force · 3.5 ft decel · 6 ft free fall · SRL 3,000 lb · connectors 5,000 / 3,600 proof Net ≤ 30 ft below; 400-lb drop test · Covers 2× load, "HOLE"/"COVER" · no storage within 6 ft of edge without guardrail

HOW TO REASON THROUGH IT 1) Is the edge ≥ 6 ft? 2) What is the slope: ≤ 4:12 or > 4:12? 3) Is it "roofing work" on a low-slope roof? If so, the menu is wider. 4) Roof width ≤ 50 ft? 5) Is mechanical equipment in use? 6) Is it residential construction? Then consider the fall protection plan path. 7) For a PFAS question, check anchorage, free fall, clearance, and component rules in that order.

1.4 Training and fall protection plans [OSHA]

  • 1926.503: the employer trains each employee exposed to fall hazards, through a competent person. Training covers hazard recognition, procedures for erecting, maintaining, disassembling, and inspecting systems, the monitor and warning-line roles, handling and storage of materials, and each employee's role.
  • Written certification record: employee name, date of training, and signature of the trainer or employer.
  • Retraining when: workplace changes make prior training obsolete, fall protection systems change, or an employee shows inadequate understanding or skill.
  • Fall protection plan (1926.502(k)): allowed only for leading-edge work, precast concrete, and residential construction, when conventional protection is infeasible or creates a greater hazard. Requirements:
  • prepared by a qualified person, site-specific, kept current, with a copy at the jobsite;
  • changes approved by a qualified person, and implementation supervised by a competent person;
  • must document why conventional fall protection is infeasible, identify controlled access zones, and use a safety monitoring system where no other measure is used;
  • the employer must identify each employee designated to work in controlled access zones;
  • serious injuries and near misses require investigation and a plan review.

Worked calculation: ladder setup

Eave height 16 ft above grade. How far out should the foot be, and what working length is needed?

  1. Foot distance = 16 ft ÷ 4 = 4 ft from the wall.
  2. Length to the eave = √(16² + 4²) = √(256 + 16) = √272 = 16.49 ft.
  3. Add 3 ft extension above the landing → ≈ 19.5 ft working length.
  4. Extension ladders lose length to section overlap (ANSI: ~3 ft overlap for ladders up to 36 ft). A 24-ft extension ladder gives ≈ 21 ft working length, so it works. A 20-ft ladder gives ≈ 17 ft, which is too short.

Common wrong answers

  • "Extend 3 rungs" or "extend 36 in above the gutter" (36 in = 3 ft is correct. The trap is "3 rungs," which is only ~3 ft if rungs are 12 in apart, and is not the rule's wording).
  • "1:3 angle." No, it's 1:4.
  • "Aluminum ladder near power lines." Prohibited when contact is possible.

1.7 Electrical hazards [OSHA]

Plain English

Roofers are killed by overhead power lines (ladders, metal panels, conveyors, and hoists touching lines) and by damaged cords and tools.

Rule Value
Work near energized parts Don't permit work where contact is possible unless the circuit is de-energized and grounded or guarded (1926.416(a)(1))
Clearance from overhead lines (unqualified workers / equipment) Commonly 10 ft for lines up to 50 kV, more for higher voltage (cranes/derricks: 1926.1408 Table A; general industry: 1910.333(c)(3), +4 in per 10 kV over 50 kV). Treat every line as energized until the utility confirms otherwise

1.8 Power tools, pneumatic nailers, and material handling [OSHA / NIOSH]

  • Pneumatic tools: secured to the hose by a positive means to prevent disconnection (1926.302(b)(1)). Compressed air may be used for cleaning only when reduced to less than 30 psi, and only with effective chip guarding and PPE (1926.302(b)(4)).
  • Nail guns (OSHA/NIOSH "Nail Gun Safety," 2011): a full sequential trigger is the safest. Contact (bump) triggers roughly double the injury risk (double-fire and unintended fire). Other practices: never bypass the safety, disconnect air when clearing jams or moving, never point at people, and keep a hand away from the nail path.
  • Lifting / ergonomics [NRCA-P]: use hoists or conveyors for bundles, lift with the legs, carry one bundle at a time, and use team lifts for rolls.
  • Conveyors / ladder hoists: lockout, guards on pinch points, and nobody under the load.

Worked calculation: noise dose (1926.52 method)

A roofer runs a power roof cutter at 100 dBA for 1 h and works near a compressor at 95 dBA for 3 h. Permitted times: 100 dBA = 2 h, 95 dBA = 4 h. Dose = C₁/T₁ + C₂/T₂ = 1/2 + 3/4 = 0.50 + 0.75 = 1.25. That is greater than 1.0, so the exposure exceeds the PEL.

1.10 Hazard communication and hazardous materials

Hazard Communication (1926.59 → 1910.1200, GHS-aligned) [OSHA]

  • SDS: 16 sections in a fixed order. Examples: Sec. 2 Hazard ID, Sec. 4 First aid, Sec. 7 Handling/storage, Sec. 8 Exposure controls/PPE, Sec. 9 Physical/chemical properties (e.g., flash point).
  • Label elements: product identifier, signal word, hazard statement(s), pictogram(s), precautionary statement(s), and name/address/phone of the manufacturer or importer.
  • Signal words: "Danger" for the more severe hazards, "Warning" for the less severe.
  • The employer needs a written hazcom program, a chemical inventory, SDSs readily accessible on every shift, and training before the initial assignment and when a new hazard is introduced.
  • [EDITION] OSHA updated the HCS in 2024 (effective July 19, 2024, with phased compliance dates in 2026–2027). The core elements above are unchanged.

1.11 Hot work: asphalt kettles, torches, propane, and fire

Hot asphalt [NRCA-P]

  • EVT (equiviscous temperature): the temperature at which asphalt reaches the viscosity for proper application. Apply at EVT ± 25°F (mopping; mechanical spreader EVTs differ). Asphalt that is too cool gives poor adhesion and heavy moppings. Asphalt that is too hot drains off, loses properties, and increases fume and fire risk.
  • Flash point (FP): never heat asphalt to or above its flash point. The common industry rule is to keep kettle temperature at least 25°F below FP; check the bill of lading or SDS [MANUFACTURER].
  • Finished blowing temperature (FBT), listed on the bill of lading: do not hold asphalt above the FBT for extended periods.
  • Kettle safety: a trained operator stays with the kettle. Keep lids closed (close them to smother a kettle fire; never use water on an asphalt fire). Keep the kettle level, away from building openings and air intakes, with an extinguisher nearby. Use a thermometer, and keep the kettle away from combustibles. [JURISDICTION] Fire codes (e.g., IFC §303) add separation distances and extinguisher ratings, and generally prohibit kettles on roofs.
  • Burn first aid [NRCA-P]: cool with cool (not ice-cold) water. Do not try to peel asphalt off the skin or use solvents on it. Get medical care.

What the roofer sees / decision tested

  • Before starting: is the deck dry, clean, smooth, securely fastened, free of ridges, depressions, and debris, and adequately sloped to drains?
  • If defects are found: document them and notify the owner or general contractor in writing before proceeding. Many contracts treat starting work as acceptance of the substrate. This is INFERENCE from standard specification language; check your contract. The roofer does not decide structural adequacy, but should not roof over visibly deteriorated, wet, or overstressed decking.
  • Deflection and ponding: a deck that sags between supports will pond water. A new membrane doesn't fix that. The fix is structural (framing/deck) or tapered insulation/crickets, not heavier roofing.

Common wrong answers

  • "The roofer is responsible for the structural design of the deck." The designer and the owner are.
  • "Mop the base sheet solid to LWIC." LWIC base sheets are mechanically fastened, because trapped moisture must escape.
  • "Any insulation can go over a steel deck." It must span the flute opening without crushing; thin or soft boards fail under foot traffic.

2.2 Tear-off vs. re-cover, and moisture diagnosis before reroofing

Code limits on re-cover (IRC R908 / IBC 1511, 2018–2021 model codes) [JURISDICTION]

A new covering may not be installed over an existing one when:

  1. the existing roof or covering is water-soaked or has deteriorated so it can't serve as a base;
  2. the existing covering is slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile;
  3. there are already two or more applications of any type of covering.

There are narrow exceptions (e.g., certain metal systems, or a new separate structural system). Local amendments vary.

NRCA's position [NRCA-P]: complete tear-off is generally preferred. It lets you inspect the deck and remove wet materials. Re-cover is an owner/designer decision based on a condition survey.

Moisture surveys (low-slope)

Method How it works Notes
Infrared thermography Wet insulation holds heat. After a sunny day, wet areas show warmer at dusk/night Needs a dry surface, low wind, and a good solar load. Ballast and reflective surfaces limit it
Nuclear moisture gauge Counts hydrogen (water) in the assembly with a neutron source; readings on a grid Licensed operator. Hydrogen in some insulations (e.g., foam, asphalt) raises baseline readings
Electrical capacitance / impedance Measures the dielectric change from moisture, through the membrane Fast; shallow reading depth; affected by surface water and conductive materials

Decision tested: the best way to confirm wet insulation found by an infrared scan is a core cut at the suspect area. Rescanning or "wait for the next rain" are weaker answers.

Worked calculation: point load from a pallet

A pallet holds 42 bundles of laminated shingles at ~75 lb each [MANUFACTURER: weights vary 60–90 lb]. The pallet footprint is 4 ft × 4 ft.

  1. Total load = 42 × 75 lb = 3,150 lb
  2. Area = 4 ft × 4 ft = 16 sq ft
  3. Pressure = 3,150 ÷ 16 = ≈ 197 psf

A typical residential roof design live load is 20 psf, and snow load may be 20–30 psf in Illinois [JURISDICTION]. A load of ~200 psf is roughly 10× that design load. That's why bundles are distributed across the roof and placed near bearing points. It isn't proof of failure (structures carry safety factors), but it is the reasoning the question is testing.

Rule of logic: higher slope and hotter climate → higher softening point, to prevent slippage (plies sliding downslope). Exact slope limits per type depend on the reference edition and the manufacturer. Learn the ordering above for certain; treat slope cut-offs as [EDITION/MANUFACTURER].

Coal tar (ASTM D450 Type I, Type III)

  • Excellent on dead-level roofs (cold-flows and self-heals; very water-resistant).
  • Incompatible with asphalt. Don't mix coal tar and asphalt materials without the manufacturer's approved separation.
  • Coal-tar fumes are more irritating and hazardous. Minimum-slope rules differ (IBC: coal-tar BUR min. ⅛:12; other membranes ¼:12) [JURISDICTION].

Related asphalt products

  • ASTM D41 asphalt primer: applied to concrete and metal surfaces before hot asphalt or adhesives, to improve adhesion.
  • Roof cement / flashing cement (ASTM D4586, asbestos-free): trowel-grade, used for repairs and flashing. Not a substitute for proper metal/membrane flashings.
  • Cutbacks (solvent-thinned) vs. emulsions (water-based). Emulsions must not freeze before curing.

Hot-asphalt terms (see §1 for safety)

  • EVT: apply at EVT ± 25°F.
  • Flash point: never reach it.
  • FBT (finished blowing temperature): don't hold above FBT for long.

Heating asphalt too hot or too long lowers its softening point (it degrades the asphalt), which raises slippage risk. That is a classic "most likely cause" item.

Installation practice [NRCA-P]

  • Two or more layers with joints staggered/offset between layers. This reduces thermal bridging and air paths through joints.
  • Don't install more insulation than can be covered with membrane the same day. At day's end, install temporary water cut-offs / night seals and remove them before resuming, so they don't become buried dams.
  • Attachment: mechanically fastened (screws and plates, with the pattern set by wind zone), adhered (hot asphalt for compatible boards, or low-rise foam adhesive in beads), or loose-laid under ballast.
  • Cover board over foam insulation is recommended under adhered membranes and where traffic, hail, or fire rating matters.

Worked calculation: total R-value

An assembly has two layers of 2.0-in polyiso at R-5.7/in, plus a ½-in gypsum cover board at R-0.5. Is it R-25?

  1. Polyiso: 2 layers × 2.0 in × 5.7 = 22.8
  2. Cover board: 0.5
  3. Total ≈ 23.3. That is short of R-25 by 1.7.
  4. Extra polyiso needed = 1.7 ÷ 5.7 = 0.30 in. In practice you'd go to a thicker layer (e.g., 2.0 in + 2.5 in = 4.5 in → 25.65 + 0.5 = 26.2).

(Energy-code R-values for commercial roofs come from the Illinois Energy Conservation Code / IECC [JURISDICTION/EDITION].)

Worked calculation: tapered insulation

A drain sits at the low point, and the high point is 36 ft away. The slope is ¼ in per ft.

  1. Rise = 36 ft × 0.25 in/ft = 9 in
  2. With a 1-in minimum starter thickness at the drain, the high-side thickness = 1 + 9 = 10 in of taper, plus any flat base layers.
  3. At ⅛ in/ft the rise would be only 4.5 in. But ¼:12 is the common minimum for positive drainage (IBC 1507 for membranes) [JURISDICTION], and NRCA recommends designing for ¼:12.

2.7 Vapor retarders, air barriers, and condensation basics

Definitions (model-code classes) [JURISDICTION]

Class Permeance Examples
Class I ≤ 0.1 perm Polyethylene sheet, foil facers, some modified-bitumen vapor retarders
Class II > 0.1 to ≤ 1.0 perm Kraft facing, some self-adhered membranes
Class III > 1.0 to ≤ 10 perm Latex paint

Rules of thumb

  • Location: on the warm-in-winter side of the insulation. In an Illinois heated building, that is below the insulation, on the deck side.
  • When one is needed on a low-slope roof [NRCA-P, widely cited]: consider a vapor retarder when the outside mean January temperature is below 40°F and interior winter RH is 45% or greater. Always evaluate high-humidity occupancies (pools, laundries, paper mills, food processing, wet manufacturing). All of Illinois has January means below 40°F. [EDITION]: recent NRCA guidance leans on a dew-point/hygrothermal analysis by the designer.
  • Air leakage moves far more moisture than vapor diffusion. Unsealed deck penetrations and perimeter gaps cause condensation. The 2026 NRCA volume title itself, "…Condensation and Air Leakage Control," reflects that emphasis.
  • Over steel decks: install a substrate board (e.g., gypsum) on the flutes first, then the vapor retarder, then insulation.
  • Dew point: the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water condenses. If any layer in the assembly is colder than the indoor air's dew point, and moist air can reach it, condensation forms there.

Diagnosis pattern: drips or ceiling stains in cold, dry weather that stop when it warms up, with no rain → condensation/air leakage, not a membrane leak.

2.10 Estimating quantities: the core math (Atcheson-style; methods are standard)

Key units

  • 1 square = 100 sq ft of roof surface.
  • Slope = rise per 12 in of run (e.g., 6:12). Pitch = rise ÷ span (6:12 slope = ¼ pitch). Tests mix the two; don't.

Slope (pitch) factors: roof area = plan area × factor

Factor = √(rise² + 12²) ÷ 12

Slope 3:12 4:12 5:12 6:12 7:12 8:12 9:12 10:12 12:12
Factor 1.031 1.054 1.083 1.118 1.158 1.202 1.250 1.302 1.414

Hip/valley length factor (per foot of common-rafter run, for equal slopes) = √(rise² + 288) ÷ 12. For 6:12: √(36 + 288) = √324 = 18 → 1.5.

Worked calculation A: squares and bundles

A gable roof has a plan area (including overhangs) of 40 ft × 30 ft, a slope of 6:12, and a 10% waste allowance. The shingles run 3 bundles per square.

  1. Plan area = 40 × 30 = 1,200 sq ft
  2. Roof area = 1,200 × 1.118 = 1,341.6 sq ft = 13.42 squares
  3. Add 10% waste: 13.42 × 1.10 = 14.76 squares → order 15 squares
  4. Bundles = 15 × 3 = 45 bundles

(Waste allowances vary by roof complexity. Roughly 10% for simple gables and 15%+ for cut-up hip roofs is a common industry rule of thumb, not a code figure.)

Worked calculation B: shingles and nails per square

  • 3-tab 12 in × 36 in at 5-in exposure: exposed area per shingle = 5 × 36 = 180 sq in. Shingles per square = 14,400 ÷ 180 = 80. At 4 nails each = 320 nails/sq; at 6 nails (high-wind) = 480 nails/sq.
  • Metric laminate 13¼ in × 39⅜ in at 5⅝-in exposure: 5.625 × 39.375 = 221.5 sq in → 14,400 ÷ 221.5 = ≈ 65 shingles/sq.

Worked calculation C: ridge cap

A 40-ft ridge with 12-in cap pieces at 5-in exposure: 40 ft × 12 in/ft ÷ 5 in = 96 pieces. With 3 pieces cut per 3-tab shingle → 32 shingles. (Laminated ridge products list their own linear-foot coverage per bundle [MANUFACTURER].)

Worked calculation D: drip edge

Same roof as calculation A (the 40 × 30 ft plan already includes overhangs, so each half-span is 15 ft horizontal).

  1. Eaves = 2 × 40 ft = 80 ft
  2. Rakes: 15 ft × 1.118 = 16.77 ft each, × 4 rake edges = 67.1 ft
  3. Total = 147.1 LF
  4. 10-ft pieces lapped 2 in give a net 9.83 ft each → 147.1 ÷ 9.83 = 14.96 → 15 pieces (most estimators would order 16 to allow for cutting waste).

Worked calculation E: ice barrier width (code version) [JURISDICTION]

The IRC requires the ice barrier to extend from the eave to at least 24 in inside the exterior wall line (R905.1.2). Overhang = 12 in horizontal; slope = 6:12.

  1. Horizontal distance = 12 in (overhang) + 24 in = 36 in
  2. Along the slope: 36 × 1.118 = 40.2 in
  3. One 36-in-wide course is not enough. You need a second course (lapped per the manufacturer).

Some manufacturers and designers measure from the interior face of the wall. That adds the wall thickness and is more conservative. Read the stem.

Worked calculation F: underlayment rolls

ASTM D226 Type I ("No. 15") rolls nominally cover 4 squares. Type II ("No. 30") rolls cover about 2 squares. Synthetic rolls vary (commonly 10 squares) [MANUFACTURER].

  • 15 squares of No. 30 → 15 ÷ 2 = 7.5 → 8 rolls. For a low-slope double layer (see §3), double it → 15 rolls.

Worked calculation G: BUR materials (NRCA-style rates) [EDITION/MANUFACTURER]

A 4-ply glass-fiber BUR on insulation, 100 squares, with a gravel surface.

  • Interply mopping: ≈ 25 lb asphalt per square per mopping (NRCA commonly cites 20–25 lb ± 15%). With 4 moppings (base + 3 interply): 4 × 25 × 100 = 10,000 lb
  • Flood coat: ≈ 60 lb/sq (asphalt) → 6,000 lb
  • Aggregate: ≈ 400 lb/sq gravel (≈ 300 lb/sq slag) → 40,000 lb = 20 tons

3.4 Layout, offsets, and appearance problems

  • 3-tab: the common offset is 6 in (cutouts not aligned; they repeat every other course).
  • Laminated: random or a minimum offset per the manufacturer (often ≥ 4–6 in).
  • "Racking" (straight-up) installation: stepping shingles straight up the roof. Many manufacturers prohibit or discourage it, because it shows color shading and puts fastener lines on top of each other. The recommended method is diagonal (stair-step) application [MANUFACTURER].
  • Shading / color variation: mixing production lots or racking. Use bundles from the same lot and blend from several bundles.
  • Chalk lines: snap lines to keep courses straight. Check exposure every few courses.

3.6 Flashing: walls, chimneys, penetrations

Step flashing (sidewall)

  • L-shaped pieces, one per shingle course, woven into the courses.
  • Size: min 4 × 4 in by code (IRC R905.2.8.3). NRCA/manufacturer practice uses pieces about 2 in longer than the exposure (e.g., ~7 in long for a 5-in exposure) with legs ~4–5 in.
  • Each piece laps the one below by ≥ 2 in.
  • Nail only the roof leg, near the top, into the deck. Never nail the wall leg or through both legs. That lets the roof and wall move independently.
  • Counterflashing (siding, a separate counterflashing, or the wall's weather-resistive barrier and trim) covers the vertical legs. On lapped siding, keep the siding clear of the roof surface per the manufacturer (often 1–2 in).
  • Continuous "L" flashing instead of steps is wrong on a shingle sidewall. Water running down the vertical leg enters the laps.

Kickout (diverter) flashing [JURISDICTION: IRC R903.2.1]

  • Required where the roof-wall intersection ends before the wall does (the eave meets a wall that continues).
  • It diverts water into the gutter instead of behind the siding.
  • A missing kickout is a classic cause of rotted wall sheathing below a roof-wall intersection.

Headwall (front/apron) flashing

  • Where the roof slopes up to a wall.
  • Continuous apron flashing: min 4 in up the wall and 4 in onto the shingles (practice varies).
  • Counterflashed by the wall cladding.

Chimney flashing

  • Components:
  • Apron on the downslope face.
  • Steps on the sides.
  • Back (upslope) flashing or cricket.

  • Counterflashing set into the mortar joints (a reglet cut about 1–1½ in deep and sealed), lapping the base/step flashing.

  • Cricket (saddle): required on the upslope side of any chimney or penetration more than 30 in wide measured perpendicular to the slope (IRC R903.2.2 / R1003.20) [JURISDICTION]. It diverts water around the chimney.

  • Why two pieces (base + counter)? The chimney and the roof move independently. The base flashing is fastened to the roof; the counterflashing is fastened to the masonry and simply laps over it.

Pipe/vent flashings

  • The flange's upper portion goes under the shingles above, and the lower portion goes over the shingles below.
  • Boots/collars are sized to the pipe. Lead or neoprene boots.
  • Plastic or rubber collars crack from UV. That is a common leak source on 10–15-year-old roofs.

Skylights

  • Use the manufacturer's flashing kit (step or continuous side flashings, head and sill flashings). The same laps and sequencing logic applies.

3.7 Hips and ridges

  • Hip and ridge shingles (manufactured, or cut 3-tab pieces 12 × 12 in) at ~5-in exposure.
  • Hips are capped from the bottom (eave) up.
  • Ridge courses: a common practice is to start at the end opposite the prevailing wind so the laps face away from it [NRCA-P/MANUFACTURER].
  • Ridge vent: the deck is cut back per the vent manufacturer (typically about 1 in each side of the ridge, or 1¾ in at a ridge board). The vent is covered with cap shingles using longer nails to reach the deck.

3.8 Reroofing over existing asphalt shingles [JURISDICTION / NRCA-P]

  • Allowed by the model codes if there is only one existing layer and it is not wet or deteriorated (see §2.2).
  • The existing roof must be flattened (curled shingles cut, and so on).
  • Replace flashings as needed; drip edge is often added.
  • Use longer nails to penetrate the deck ¾ in.
  • The "nesting" technique is used for 3-tab over 3-tab: new courses butt against the old course lines.
  • NRCA prefers tear-off (deck inspection, less weight, better performance).

Worked calculation: panel expansion

A 40-ft panel sees a 150°F surface-temperature swing (−10°F winter night to 140°F summer surface).

  1. L = 40 ft × 12 = 480 in
  2. Steel: 6.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 480 × 150 = 0.468 in (≈ ½ in)
  3. Aluminum: 12.8 × 10⁻⁶ × 480 × 150 = 0.922 in (≈ 1 in)

Aluminum moves about twice as much as steel. That's why long panel runs need sliding clips and a fixed point, and why exposed-fastener panels on long runs leak at the screws.

3.14 Attic ventilation (steep-slope residential)

Why ventilate [NRCA-P / IRC]

  • Removes moisture (preventing condensation, mold, and wet sheathing).
  • Reduces summer heat (longer shingle life, lower cooling load).
  • Reduces ice dams (keeps the roof deck cold in winter, along with air sealing and insulation).
  • Shingle warranties often require code-compliant ventilation [MANUFACTURER].

Code minimum (IRC R806.2) [JURISDICTION]

  • The net free ventilating area (NFA) must be at least 1/150 of the vented attic floor area.
  • It may be reduced to 1/300 when either of these is met: 1. In Climate Zones 6, 7, 8, a Class I or II vapor retarder is installed on the warm-in-winter side of the ceiling. (Illinois is CZ 4–5, so this route rarely applies here.) 2. At least 40% and not more than 50% of the required area is in the upper portion (exhaust, within 3 ft of the ridge), and the rest is in the eave/soffit (intake).

Worked calculation: ventilation

The attic floor is 1,800 sq ft, using the 1/300 option with a balanced intake/exhaust split.

  1. Required NFA = 1,800 ÷ 300 = 6 sq ft = 6 × 144 = 864 sq in
  2. 50% exhaust = 432 sq in; 50% intake = 432 sq in
  3. A ridge vent rated 18 sq in NFA per linear foot [MANUFACTURER] → 432 ÷ 18 = 24 LF of ridge vent
  4. Using 1/150 instead: 1,800 ÷ 150 = 12 sq ft = 1,728 sq in

Ventilation rules of thumb (the "best answer" logic)

  • Balanced: intake ≥ exhaust. Many manufacturers want intake ≥ exhaust so the exhaust vents don't pull conditioned air or weather in [MANUFACTURER].
  • Don't mix exhaust types in the same attic (e.g., ridge vent plus gable louvers or a power fan). The second exhaust short-circuits the flow, and the ridge vent can pull rain and snow in through the other exhaust.
  • Baffles (vent chutes) keep insulation off the soffit vents and maintain an air channel (IRC R806.3 requires at least 1 in of air space between the insulation and the sheathing at the vent).
  • Bath and kitchen fans must exhaust to the outdoors, never into the attic.
  • Net free area is the actual open area. It's always less than the vent's gross size (screens and louvers block part of it).
  • Unvented (conditioned) attics are allowed under specific rules (IRC R806.5): air-impermeable insulation at the roof deck, or equivalent [JURISDICTION].

4.2 Built-up roofing (BUR)

Components, bottom to top

  1. Deck (with a vapor retarder if required)
  2. Insulation (plus cover board)
  3. Base sheet
  4. Ply sheets (commonly 3–4 plies of glass-fiber felts, ASTM D2178 Type IV/VI)
  5. Interply bitumen between every ply
  6. Surfacing: - flood coat + aggregate (gravel/slag); - cap sheet (mineral-surfaced); - smooth surface + coating.

Application essentials [NRCA-P]

  • Shingle the plies: each ply is lapped so the finished roof has the specified number of plies at every point. A 4-ply roof has 4 plies everywhere, including along the edges and laps.
  • No felt-to-felt contact. Mop each ply fully and broom it in so it is completely embedded. Voids cause blisters.
  • Asphalt at EVT ± 25°F. Too cool → poor adhesion and voids. Too hot → the asphalt degrades, its softening point drops, and it can slip.
  • Avoid phased application (putting the base plies on one day and finishing weeks later). Moisture, dust, and dirt get trapped between the phases → blisters and delamination. NRCA discourages it.
  • Backnailing plies to a nailer at the high edge prevents slippage on steeper low-slope roofs. The slope threshold depends on the edition/manufacturer [EDITION]; the concept is certain.
  • Aggregate: embedded in a flood coat (≈ 60 lb/sq asphalt) at ≈ 400 lb/sq gravel or 300 lb/sq slag. It protects against UV, fire, and hail.
  • Cant strips (45°) at wall and curb junctions. Bituminous plies and flashings crack if bent 90°.

Defect What you see Most likely cause

Splitting A straight tear through all plies Thermal/structural stress concentration (at insulation joints or deck changes), inadequate reinforcement

4.3 Modified bitumen

Item SBS (styrene-butadiene-styrene) APP (atactic polypropylene)
Nature Elastomeric (rubbery); flexible in cold Plastomeric (plastic); good heat and UV tolerance
Application Hot-mopped asphalt, cold adhesive, self-adhered, or torch Torch-applied / heat-welded (the classic), some cold-applied
Surfacing Granules (cap sheet), or coated Often smooth + coating, or granules
  • Reinforcement:
  • Polyester gives elongation and puncture resistance.

  • Glass gives dimensional stability.

  • Typical system: a 2-ply base sheet + cap sheet. Stagger the cap sheet's side laps from the base sheet's side laps.

  • Laps (typical) [MANUFACTURER]:
  • side laps ~3 in, end laps ~6 in;
  • stagger end laps (e.g., ≥ 3 ft apart);

  • cut a diagonal "dog-ear" at the end-lap corner where it meets the side lap.

  • Torching: heat the roll and the substrate until the bitumen flows. A uniform bleed-out of about ¼–½ in at laps shows proper fusion [MANUFACTURER].

  • No bleed-out → a cold, unfused lap.

  • Excessive bleed-out → overheating (and wasted modified asphalt).

  • Granules at end laps: embed the granules (heat and press them in, or use a granule-free lap edge) before lapping, so the bitumen bonds to bitumen.

  • Cold weather: warm the rolls (store them heated) to prevent cracking and help them relax.

Fire: torching is limited near combustible decks, concealed spaces, and flashings; follow NRCA's CERTA rules (see §1.11: 2-h fire watch).

Welding practice [MANUFACTURER / NRCA-P]

  • Test welds at the start of each day and whenever conditions change (temperature, wind, humidity, a new power source). Peel-test the sample:
  • Good weld: the failure happens in the membrane, outside the weld (film tearing / scrim exposure), not at the weld interface.
  • Cold weld: too little heat or too fast; the seam peels apart cleanly.

  • Burnt weld: too much heat or too slow; the membrane is scorched or degraded.

  • Weld width: the manufacturer's minimum is commonly 1½ in for automatic welders (hand welds are often wider) [MANUFACTURER].

  • Probe every seam with a blunt probe (e.g., a cotter-pin puller) after the weld cools. Repair voids with a patch.
  • T-joints in thicker membranes (where three layers meet) often need a T-joint cover patch [MANUFACTURER].
  • Aged membrane: clean with the manufacturer's weathered-membrane cleaner before welding repairs.
  • Cut edges of reinforced sheets are sealed per the manufacturer (sealant/edge treatment for some products).

Wind zones

  • Corners see the highest uplift, then perimeters, then the field.
  • Enhanced attachment in corners and perimeters: more fasteners per board, closer seam or fastener spacing, half sheets.
  • Design pressures come from ASCE 7. Tested assemblies are rated by FM Approvals (e.g., Class 1-60, 1-90 = psf uplift ratings) or UL 580/1897 [JURISDICTION/FM].

4.7 Repair, maintenance, and leak/moisture diagnosis

Maintenance [NRCA-P / Repair Manual scope]

  • Inspect at least twice a year (spring and fall) and after major storms.
  • Keep drains, scuppers, and gutters clear. Check flashings, counterflashings, sealants, seams, pitch pans, and walkways. Look for ponding and vegetation.
  • Temporary (emergency) repairs stop water now: cold-applied mastic/tape compatible with the membrane. Permanent repairs follow later with matching materials.

Repair methods by membrane (typical)

Membrane Repair
BUR Blister: cut it out or X-cut it, dry it, re-adhere, and patch with plies extending well beyond the cut (commonly ~6 in). Remove gravel and prime before patching. Don't cut blisters that aren't leaking just because they exist; evaluate first
Mod-bit Clean, heat or prime, patch with compatible mod-bit, and embed granules at the laps
EPDM Clean → prime → cured cover tape/patch with rounded corners
TPO/PVC Clean (weathered-membrane cleaner) → weld a patch of the same material, rounded corners, probe

Finding the leak

  1. Interview and map: where it leaks inside, when (rain only? wind direction? snowmelt? cold dry days?), and since when.
  2. Look above and upslope of the interior drip. Water travels along the deck, fasteners, and insulation joints before it shows.
  3. Check the usual suspects first: flashings, counterflashings, penetrations, drains, seams, and roof edges.
  4. Water test: start low and work up, flooding one component at a time, so you know which one leaked.

  5. Electronic leak detection: - Low-voltage EFVM (electric field vector mapping) needs a conductive deck/surface path and a wet surface; - high-voltage (spark) testing works on a dry surface over a conductive substrate.

  6. Moisture survey (IR, nuclear, capacitance) plus core cuts to find wet insulation that must be replaced (see §2.2).

Leak or condensation?

Clue Points to
Wet only during/after rain, worse with wind from one direction Leak (flashing, seam, penetration)
Wet on cold days without rain; frost or water on the underside of the deck; worse in winter over humid spaces Condensation (vapor or air leakage; missing or failed vapor retarder or air seal)
Leaks when snow melts on sunny days Ice/snow melt at a flashing, drain, or ice-dam equivalent (blocked drains or scuppers)

4.8 Worked calculations

A. Ballast load

A 100-square ballasted EPDM roof at 10 psf of ballast:

  • 100 sq × 100 sq ft/sq × 10 lb/sq ft = 100,000 lb = 50 tons. The structure must be able to carry it [verify with the structural engineer].

B. Mechanically attached fastener count (field)

10-ft-wide sheets with 6-in seam laps, fasteners at 12 in o.c. in the laps.

  1. Net coverage per sheet = 10 ft − 0.5 ft = 9.5 ft
  2. One fastener row per 9.5 ft of roof width, with a fastener every 1 ft along the row
  3. Fasteners per sq ft = 1 ÷ (9.5 × 1) = 0.105 → ≈ 10.5 per square in the field
  4. For 80 field squares → ≈ 842 fasteners (perimeter and corner zones need more, e.g., half-width sheets double the rows)

C. Drain sump / slope rise

With tapered insulation at ¼:12 and 30 ft from the drain to the high point: rise = 30 × 0.25 = 7.5 in.

D. Mod-bit coverage with laps (rolls per square)

A cap roll is 39⅜ in × 32.8 ft (≈ 1 square gross) [MANUFACTURER], with 3-in side laps and 6-in end laps.

  • Net width = 39.375 − 3 = 36.375 in = 3.03 ft
  • Net length = 32.8 − 0.5 = 32.3 ft
  • Net ≈ 3.03 × 32.3 ≈ 98 sq ft → ~1 roll per net square, plus waste. That's why mod-bit rolls are marketed as "1 square" (gross) and estimators add waste.

WHAT TO MEMORIZE (Section 4) Min slope ¼:12 (coal tar ⅛:12) · ponding = water remaining 48 h after rain · thermoset EPDM = tape/adhesive seams; thermoplastic TPO/PVC = hot-air welds · adhesive on both surfaces (EPDM fully adhered) · ballast ~10–12 psf BUR: 3–4 plies shingled, no felt-to-felt, EVT ± 25°F, no phased application, flood 60 lb + gravel 400 / slag 300 lb per sq, cant strips for bituminous Defects: blister = voids/moisture between plies · slippage = wrong asphalt/too much/overheated/no backnailing · alligatoring = UV on bare asphalt · ridging = insulation joint movement SBS = elastomeric (mop/cold/self-adhered/torch); APP = plastomeric (torch) · mod-bit side lap ~3 in, end lap ~6 in, stagger ≥ 3 ft, ¼–½ in bleed-out TPO/PVC: test welds daily and when conditions change · good peel = membrane tears outside the weld · probe cooled seams · weld width ~1½ in · PVC ✕ asphalt/coal tar/EPS · PVC best for grease Base flashing ≥ 8 in · counterflashing laps ~4 in · curbs ≥ 8 in · pitch pans are a last resort · penetrations ~12 in apart · drain in a sump, clamping ring tight · corners > perimeters > field for uplift · SPRI ES-1 edges

HOW TO REASON THROUGH LOW-SLOPE ITEMS - Identify the membrane family first (thermoset, thermoplastic, bituminous). The seam method, flashing method, compatibility, and repair method all follow from it. - For a defect item, ask whether the cause is bitumen chemistry (type/temperature), workmanship (voids, laps, welds), movement (joints, deck), or moisture (trapped water, condensation). - For a detail item, the best answer keeps the top of the waterproofing above water level (8-in rule) and covered (counterflashing).

A3. Qualifying party (QP) rules [IL-V; EDITION: rewritten 8-15-2025]

  • Who: the individual who takes the exam for the company. The QP is legally qualified to act for the business in all roofing matters, has authority to supervise installation, and is actively engaged in day-to-day activities.
  • Employment (§3(3.5)): the QP must be an employee. The employer deducts FICA and withholding and provides workers' comp. The QP may not receive a Form 1099 from the licensee.
  • One QP per licensee at all times (§4.5(b)).
  • One licensee per QP (§4.5(a)). Exception: the QP may serve one additional licensee of the same license type if there's common ownership or management (≥ 25%) or it's a licensed subsidiary (≥ 25% owned or managed).

  • When a QP leaves (§4.6): 1. Both the licensee and the QP notify the Department in writing (mail or email) within 30 business days of the disassociation. Otherwise the license goes inoperative. 2. The licensee names a new QP within 60 days after notifying the Department. Otherwise the license goes inoperative. 3. The new QP must take and pass the exam within 7 months after Department approval. Otherwise the license is automatically inoperative until the company requalifies.

  • [EDITION] The older Rule 1460.11(e), from 2021, says 30 business days to name the new QP. The 2025 statute says 60 days. The statute controls. If an exam item offers only 30 business days, it was likely written to the older rule.

  2. the approximate dates of service;

  • Elements of a valid contract:
  • offer;
  • acceptance;
  • consideration;
  • legal capacity;
  • lawful purpose.

  • Contract types:

  • lump sum / fixed price: contractor bears cost risk;
  • cost-plus (with or without a GMP);

  • time and materials.

  • scope/specifications;
  • change orders (in writing, signed, before the work);
  • payment schedule and retainage;
  • liquidated damages (a pre-agreed daily amount for late completion);
  • indemnification;
  • warranties;
  • differing site conditions;
  • termination;
  • dispute resolution (mediation → arbitration → litigation);

B4. Liens, bonds, and insurance

  • Mechanics liens (Illinois Mechanics Lien Act, 770 ILCS 60) [NOT-CTS; verify details]:
  • Contractor: record the lien within 4 months after completion to be effective against third parties (buyers, lenders), and sue to enforce within 2 years after completion.
  • Subcontractors: serve notice on the owner within 90 days after completing their work. For owner-occupied residences there are shorter notices (60 days from first furnishing).

  • Before payment, the contractor gives the owner a sworn statement listing the subs and amounts, then collects lien waivers.

  • No liens on public property. That is why public projects require payment bonds.

  • Surety bonds (three parties): principal (contractor), obligee (owner), surety (bonding company). A surety is not insurance for the contractor; the contractor must reimburse the surety.
  • Bid bond: guarantees the bidder will sign the contract and furnish the other bonds (often 5–10% of the bid).
  • Performance bond: protects the owner if the contractor defaults (usually 100% of the contract).
  • Payment bond: protects subs and suppliers (usually 100%).
  • Federal Miller Act and the Illinois Public Construction Bond Act require them on public work above thresholds (verify the current thresholds).

  • The Illinois license bond ($10k/$25k) is a license/permit bond protecting the public and the State, not a performance bond.

  • Insurance:

  • CGL (commercial general liability): occurrence vs. claims-made, with completed-operations coverage (injury or damage after the job is finished);
  • commercial auto;
  • workers' compensation (with employer's liability);
  • umbrella/excess;

Part C: Financial management and estimating math

C1. Financial statements

  • Balance sheet (a snapshot on a date): Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity.
  • Income statement (a period): Revenue − Direct job costs = Gross profit; Gross profit − Overhead (indirect) = Net profit (before tax).
  • Cash-flow statement: operating, investing, and financing cash.
  • Ratios:
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities (≈ 1.5–2.0 is usually considered healthy);
  • Quick (acid-test) ratio = (cash + receivables) ÷ current liabilities;
  • Working capital = CA − CL;

  • Debt-to-equity = total liabilities ÷ equity.

  • Accounting methods:

  • cash basis vs. accrual basis;
  • for long contracts, percentage-of-completion vs. completed-contract;

  • over-billing (billed ahead of the work: a liability) vs. under-billing (work ahead of billing: an asset).

  • Depreciation (straight-line) = (cost − salvage) ÷ useful life.

C2. Markup vs. margin (the most-missed business calculation)

  • Markup is a % of cost. Margin is a % of selling price.
  • Price = Cost × (1 + markup)
  • Price = Cost ÷ (1 − margin)
  • margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup); markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

Worked calculation 1: pricing a job with overhead and profit

The company expects $240,000 annual overhead on $1,200,000 of annual direct job costs.

  1. Overhead rate = 240,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 20% of direct cost
  2. A job has direct costs of $18,000 (materials $8,000 + burdened labor $10,000). Overhead allocated = 0.20 × 18,000 = $3,600. Total cost = $21,600
  3. The target is a 10% net profit margin on the selling price: Price = 21,600 ÷ (1 − 0.10) = $24,000
  4. Check: net profit = 24,000 − 21,600 = 2,400, and 2,400 ÷ 24,000 = 10% ✔ - Gross profit = 24,000 − 18,000 = 6,000 → gross margin 25% - Markup on direct cost = 6,000 ÷ 18,000 = 33.3%

Trap: adding 10% to cost (21,600 × 1.10 = $23,760) gives a 9.1% margin, not 10%.

6.1 Commercial roof assemblies and approvals

The layered assembly, bottom to top

  1. Structural deck (steel, concrete, wood, LWIC, gypsum)
  2. Thermal barrier / substrate board (e.g., gypsum over steel, where the code or the tested assembly requires it)
  3. Vapor retarder / air barrier (when needed)
  4. Insulation: one or more layers, staggered joints
  5. Cover board
  6. Membrane
  7. Surfacing/protection: coating, granules, ballast, pavers, or a vegetative system

Topic Key facts
Steel deck Commonly 1½-in wide-rib (Type B), 22 ga minimum in NRCA/FM practice. It is attached to joists by puddle welds, screws, or power-actuated fasteners, and sidelaps are fastened. Reinforce or repair rusted or deflected areas before reroofing. Report deck damage to the owner (structural)

6.3 Vapor control in commercial and industrial buildings

High-humidity occupancies always need evaluation:

  • natatoriums (pools);
  • laundries;
  • paper mills;
  • food processing;
  • breweries;
  • textile and wet manufacturing;
  • cold-storage (freezer) buildings, where the vapor drive reverses: warm, moist air outside, cold inside.

Rules:

  • General rule: the vapor retarder goes on the warm side of the insulation.
  • Over steel deck: fasten or adhere a substrate board first, apply the vapor retarder on top of it, and then install the insulation over it.
  • Continuity matters: seal the vapor retarder at perimeters and penetrations and tie it into the wall air/vapor barrier. Air leakage at the roof-wall joint is a leading cause of condensation problems [2026 NRCA volume emphasis].
  • Cold storage: the vapor retarder goes on the exterior (warm) side of the insulation, which is usually the roof membrane itself. The assembly must be designed so that no interior vapor retarder traps moisture.

Topic Key facts
Structural standing seam (SSR) Spans purlins (no solid deck needed). Concealed clips: fixed clips at one point (often the eave, ridge, or midpoint, per design) and sliding clips elsewhere, so panels move with temperature. Mechanically field-seamed (e.g., 180° double-lock) for low slopes
Architectural standing seam Needs a solid substrate and underlayment. Steeper slopes (it sheds water)

6.5 Spray polyurethane foam (SPF) and coatings

Topic Key facts
What it is Two components (A side: isocyanate; B side: polyol resin) mixed at the gun. It expands into a seamless, adhered, insulating layer. Roofing-grade foam is roughly 2.5–3.0 lb/ft³ density [MANUFACTURER/ASTM C1029]
Protective coating Required. SPF degrades in UV. Use an elastomeric coating (acrylic, silicone, urethane) or aggregate. Apply the coating within the manufacturer's window (often the same day or within about 72 hours) [MANUFACTURER]
Weather limits Don't spray on wet or damp surfaces, in high wind (overspray and poor texture), or when the substrate is near the dew point. A common rule is surface temperature at least 5°F above the dew point [MANUFACTURER]
Surface texture Smooth / orange peel / coarse orange peel are acceptable to coat. Popcorn / tree bark is unacceptable: poor coating coverage and water holding. Remove it and re-spray
Thickness Specified for R-value and to create slope (SPF can be sprayed thicker to build drainage)
Overspray Travels far in the wind. Protect cars, windows, and HVAC (masking, wind screens, stopping work in wind)
Safety Isocyanate is a respiratory sensitizer. Use supplied-air or appropriate respirators, controlled zones, and notify occupants. Shut down or seal HVAC intakes
Maintenance Inspect the coating; recoat at the end of its service life. Blisters in SPF come from moisture, poor pass adhesion, or a wet substrate

Roof coatings in general (restoration):

  • Clean.
  • Repair wet areas: wet insulation must be removed first; don't coat over wet roofs.
  • Prime as required.
  • Reinforce seams and flashings with fabric.
  • Apply at the specified dry-film thickness (mils). Coverage is in gallons per square, and wet mils ≠ dry mils (solids content matters) [MANUFACTURER].

Worked calculation: coating coverage

A coating is 60% solids by volume. The specification requires 20 dry mils.

  1. Wet mils needed = 20 ÷ 0.60 = 33.3 wet mils
  2. 1 gallon spread over 100 sq ft gives about 16 wet mils (1 gal = 231 in³; 231 ÷ 14,400 in² = 0.016 in = 16 mils)
  3. Gallons per square = 33.3 ÷ 16 = ≈ 2.08 gal/sq (before waste)
  4. For 120 squares: 2.08 × 120 = ≈ 250 gal (+ waste)

6.8 Working on occupied commercial buildings, warranties, and quality control

  • Fumes and odors (hot asphalt, adhesives, SPF): coordinate with the owner. Shut down or seal rooftop air intakes during work, and use low-odor products where needed.
  • Tear-off in sections that can be made watertight the same day. Install water cut-offs / night seals, and remove them before continuing (§2.6).
  • Asbestos in old roofs: test before tear-off (§1.10).
  • Deck damage found during tear-off: stop, document, and notify. Unit-price items for deck replacement are common in commercial contracts.
  • Warranties:
  • Manufacturer: material-only, system, or NDL (no dollar limit). These often require a manufacturer inspection and approved-applicator status.
  • Contractor workmanship: commonly 2 years by industry convention; per the contract.

  • Warranties usually exclude damage from ponding beyond limits, unauthorized alterations, and lack of maintenance.

  • Quality control: daily test welds (§4.5), seam probing, adhesive coverage checks, fastener pull-out tests (existing decks), core cuts, and infrared or electronic leak detection at completion.

2. Nine skills a recall-only guide doesn't train

Each skill below shows a sample stem style I wrote myself to illustrate the skill. These are not CTS questions.

2.1 Picking the best answer among true statements

  • The trap: three options are acceptable and one is most correct per the reference. Example of the pattern: several OSHA-permitted options for a low-slope roof, but only one fits the stated roof width or equipment.
  • How to win: read the stem for limiters: "on a roof 50 ft or less in width," "when mechanical equipment is used," "NRCA recommends," "minimum," "maximum," "first," "most likely." The limiter decides the answer.

2.2 Sequencing roof work

  • Examples: order of underlayment and drip edge at eave vs. rake. Order of base flashing, counterflashing, and cap flashing. Closed-cut valley: which plane's shingles go first. Tear-off, deck inspection, deck repair, dry-in. BUR: base sheet, plies, flood coat, aggregate.
  • How to win: use the water-shedding rule. Every upper component laps over the component below it. Exceptions (for example, underlayment over the eave drip edge) are exactly what tests ask about. Memorize them.

2.3 Diagnosing installation errors (the "most likely cause")

  • Examples: shingle blow-off → high nailing or unsealed strips. Blisters in BUR → trapped moisture or air between plies. Membrane shrinkage pulling at the perimeter. EPDM seam failure → dirty or unprimed laps. Wet insulation at a wall base → failed counterflashing.
  • How to win: match the symptom's location and timing to the mechanism. A leak only during wind-driven rain points to flashing/laps. Ceiling stains in cold weather with no rain points to condensation or air leakage.

2.4 Material compatibility

  • Examples: PVC vs. asphalt, EPDM vs. petroleum, polystyrene vs. hot asphalt and solvents, coal tar vs. asphalt, copper runoff onto galvanized or aluminum, and treated lumber vs. aluminum.
  • How to win: keep a single "never touch" table in memory (Guide §2 and §4).

2.5 Safety decisions, not just safety numbers

  • Examples: which fall-protection system is permitted in a given situation; what to do after a fall arrest; when a safety monitor alone is legal; what's wrong with an anchorage at foot level.
  • How to win: know the structure of 29 CFR 1926.501(b) (which paragraph covers which situation), then the §1926.502 criteria.

2.6 Estimating calculations

  • Examples: plan area → pitch factor → squares → waste → bundles. Ridge/hip linear feet. Felt rolls. Ventilation NFA. Asphalt pounds per square. Thermal movement. Markup vs. margin. Labor burden.
  • How to win: write units on every line. Most wrong answers on calculation items are unit errors or markup/margin confusion.

2.7 Low-slope details

  • Examples: base flashing height, cant strips (when and why), counterflashing lap, drain/sump detailing, pitch-pan limits, ponding definition, positive-drainage slope, seam laps and weld widths, wind zones (field/perimeter/corner).
  • How to win: learn the NRCA detail logic (why each piece exists). Then numbers are easier to recall.

2.8 Commercial systems

  • Examples: fully adhered vs. mechanically attached vs. ballasted. Insulation attachment. Cover boards. Vapor retarders (when and where). Steel deck attachment. Waterproofing (below-grade and plaza) vs. roofing. SPF and coatings.

2.9 Illinois-specific and business rules

  • Illinois: limited vs. unlimited, the 8-units-or-less line, bond $10,000 / $25,000, insurance $250,000 PD / $500,000 BI (Rule 1460.20), license number on contracts, bids, ads, and commercial vehicles, contract contents (72-hour rescission, deductible statement, trust statement in bold), qualifying-party rules (one QP, 30 business days, 60 days, 7 months), fines.
  • National business (NASCLA-type): FICA/FUTA, I-9, W-2/1099, record retention, lien basics, bonds, insurance types, balance sheet and income statement, ratios, markup vs. margin, OSHA 300 log.

4. Answer-choice tactics for "BEST answer" items (INFERENCE, general test craft)

  1. Find the limiter word first (minimum, maximum, first, most likely, NRCA-recommended, OSHA-required, Illinois).
  2. Classify the question's authority. Is it asking OSHA law, NRCA practice, manufacturer instruction, Illinois law, or business math? Then answer from that authority.
  3. Eliminate "partially right, wrong scope." For example, an OSHA rule that exists but applies to scaffolds, not roofs.
  4. Between two close options, prefer the one that protects water-shedding or life-safety without exception.
  5. Absolute words ("always," "never") are neither automatically wrong nor automatically right. OSHA and NRCA really do use "never" in places (never ride a material hoist; never heat asphalt above its flash point).
  6. Calculation items: estimate the magnitude first so you can spot a unit error in the options.
  7. Time budget: 150 min ÷ 130 ≈ 69 s per item. Do a first pass in ~100 minutes, flag hard items, and use the remaining ~50 minutes for flagged items and calculations. Never leave a blank. Unanswered items score as wrong (as reported by prep providers; CTS's scoring rules for blanks are not stated in S1).
