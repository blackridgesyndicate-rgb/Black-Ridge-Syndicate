2. Nine skills a recall-only guide doesn't train

Each skill below shows a sample stem style I wrote myself to illustrate the skill. These are not CTS questions.

2.1 Picking the best answer among true statements

  • The trap: three options are acceptable and one is most correct per the reference. Example of the pattern: several OSHA-permitted options for a low-slope roof, but only one fits the stated roof width or equipment.
  • How to win: read the stem for limiters: "on a roof 50 ft or less in width," "when mechanical equipment is used," "NRCA recommends," "minimum," "maximum," "first," "most likely." The limiter decides the answer.

2.2 Sequencing roof work

  • Examples: order of underlayment and drip edge at eave vs. rake. Order of base flashing, counterflashing, and cap flashing. Closed-cut valley: which plane's shingles go first. Tear-off, deck inspection, deck repair, dry-in. BUR: base sheet, plies, flood coat, aggregate.
  • How to win: use the water-shedding rule. Every upper component laps over the component below it. Exceptions (for example, underlayment over the eave drip edge) are exactly what tests ask about. Memorize them.

2.3 Diagnosing installation errors (the "most likely cause")

  • Examples: shingle blow-off → high nailing or unsealed strips. Blisters in BUR → trapped moisture or air between plies. Membrane shrinkage pulling at the perimeter. EPDM seam failure → dirty or unprimed laps. Wet insulation at a wall base → failed counterflashing.
  • How to win: match the symptom's location and timing to the mechanism. A leak only during wind-driven rain points to flashing/laps. Ceiling stains in cold weather with no rain points to condensation or air leakage.

2.4 Material compatibility

  • Examples: PVC vs. asphalt, EPDM vs. petroleum, polystyrene vs. hot asphalt and solvents, coal tar vs. asphalt, copper runoff onto galvanized or aluminum, and treated lumber vs. aluminum.
  • How to win: keep a single "never touch" table in memory (Guide §2 and §4).

2.5 Safety decisions, not just safety numbers

  • Examples: which fall-protection system is permitted in a given situation; what to do after a fall arrest; when a safety monitor alone is legal; what's wrong with an anchorage at foot level.
  • How to win: know the structure of 29 CFR 1926.501(b) (which paragraph covers which situation), then the §1926.502 criteria.

2.6 Estimating calculations

  • Examples: plan area → pitch factor → squares → waste → bundles. Ridge/hip linear feet. Felt rolls. Ventilation NFA. Asphalt pounds per square. Thermal movement. Markup vs. margin. Labor burden.
  • How to win: write units on every line. Most wrong answers on calculation items are unit errors or markup/margin confusion.

2.7 Low-slope details

  • Examples: base flashing height, cant strips (when and why), counterflashing lap, drain/sump detailing, pitch-pan limits, ponding definition, positive-drainage slope, seam laps and weld widths, wind zones (field/perimeter/corner).
  • How to win: learn the NRCA detail logic (why each piece exists). Then numbers are easier to recall.

2.8 Commercial systems

  • Examples: fully adhered vs. mechanically attached vs. ballasted. Insulation attachment. Cover boards. Vapor retarders (when and where). Steel deck attachment. Waterproofing (below-grade and plaza) vs. roofing. SPF and coatings.

2.9 Illinois-specific and business rules

  • Illinois: limited vs. unlimited, the 8-units-or-less line, bond $10,000 / $25,000, insurance $250,000 PD / $500,000 BI (Rule 1460.20), license number on contracts, bids, ads, and commercial vehicles, contract contents (72-hour rescission, deductible statement, trust statement in bold), qualifying-party rules (one QP, 30 business days, 60 days, 7 months), fines.
  • National business (NASCLA-type): FICA/FUTA, I-9, W-2/1099, record retention, lien basics, bonds, insurance types, balance sheet and income statement, ratios, markup vs. margin, OSHA 300 log.

4. Answer-choice tactics for "BEST answer" items (INFERENCE, general test craft)

  1. Find the limiter word first (minimum, maximum, first, most likely, NRCA-recommended, OSHA-required, Illinois).
  2. Classify the question's authority. Is it asking OSHA law, NRCA practice, manufacturer instruction, Illinois law, or business math? Then answer from that authority.
  3. Eliminate "partially right, wrong scope." For example, an OSHA rule that exists but applies to scaffolds, not roofs.
  4. Between two close options, prefer the one that protects water-shedding or life-safety without exception.
  5. Absolute words ("always," "never") are neither automatically wrong nor automatically right. OSHA and NRCA really do use "never" in places (never ride a material hoist; never heat asphalt above its flash point).
  6. Calculation items: estimate the magnitude first so you can spot a unit error in the options.
  7. Time budget: 150 min ÷ 130 ≈ 69 s per item. Do a first pass in ~100 minutes, flag hard items, and use the remaining ~50 minutes for flagged items and calculations. Never leave a blank. Unanswered items score as wrong (as reported by prep providers; CTS's scoring rules for blanks are not stated in S1).

1.1 Fall protection: the framework (the most-tested safety topic)

Plain English

Anyone working 6 ft or more above a lower level on a construction site needs fall protection. What counts as acceptable depends on the roof slope, the type of work, and whether it's residential construction.

Terms you must recognize (29 CFR 1926.500(b) definitions) [OSHA]

  • Low-slope roof: slope ≤ 4 in 12 (vertical to horizontal).
  • Steep roof: slope > 4 in 12.
  • Roofing work: hoisting, storage, application, and removal of roofing materials and equipment, including related insulation, sheet metal, and vapor-barrier work. It does not include constructing the roof deck.
  • Unprotected sides and edges: any side or edge where there is no wall or guardrail at least 39 in high.
  • Hole: a gap or void 2 in or more in its least dimension.
  • Competent person (1926.32(f)): capable of identifying hazards and has authority to take prompt corrective measures.
  • Qualified person (1926.32(m)): has a recognized degree, certificate, or professional standing, or extensive knowledge, training, and experience, and can solve problems relating to the subject.

Plain English

What the roofer sees / decision tested

  • A roof anchor nailed through the sheathing only (not into a rafter/truss) → inadequate anchorage. Manufacturer instructions typically require fastening into framing [MANUFACTURER].
  • A 6-ft shock-absorbing lanyard clipped to an anchor at the worker's feet → free fall ≈ 6 ft + D-ring height (≈ 5 ft) ≈ 11 ft > 6-ft limit. Correct fix: rig the anchor overhead, shorten the lanyard, or use an SRL/lanyard rated for foot-level anchorage. The best answer depends on the options, but the flaw is the free-fall distance.
  • A worker fell and was arrested with no injury. Best next step: rescue promptly, then remove the harness and lanyard from service until a competent person inspects them. Do not simply reuse them.

Worked calculation: required clearance below the anchor

This is an industry method (ANSI/ASSP Z359 and manufacturer charts), not an OSHA formula. OSHA sets only the 6-ft free fall, 3.5-ft deceleration, and no-contact rules.

Worker with a 6-ft shock-absorbing lanyard, anchor at the D-ring level (≈ 5 ft above feet):

Step Value

Deceleration distance (OSHA max) 3.5 ft
Harness stretch / D-ring shift (industry allowance) 1.0 ft
D-ring to bottom of feet 5.0 ft
Safety margin (industry) 2.0 ft

  • "Anchorage 3,000 lb." That is the SRL (≤ 2 ft free fall) and positioning figure, not the general anchorage.
  • "Free fall 4 ft." No; the limit is 6 ft.
  • "Body belt is fine for arrest." Prohibited since 1998.
  • "Two workers can share one vertical lifeline." Prohibited.

1.4 Training and fall protection plans [OSHA]

  • 1926.503: the employer trains each employee exposed to fall hazards, through a competent person. Training covers hazard recognition, procedures for erecting, maintaining, disassembling, and inspecting systems, the monitor and warning-line roles, handling and storage of materials, and each employee's role.
  • Written certification record: employee name, date of training, and signature of the trainer or employer.
  • Retraining when: workplace changes make prior training obsolete, fall protection systems change, or an employee shows inadequate understanding or skill.
  • Fall protection plan (1926.502(k)): allowed only for leading-edge work, precast concrete, and residential construction, when conventional protection is infeasible or creates a greater hazard. Requirements:
  • prepared by a qualified person, site-specific, kept current, with a copy at the jobsite;
  • changes approved by a qualified person, and implementation supervised by a competent person;
  • must document why conventional fall protection is infeasible, identify controlled access zones, and use a safety monitoring system where no other measure is used;
  • the employer must identify each employee designated to work in controlled access zones;
  • serious injuries and near misses require investigation and a plan review.

Worked calculation: ladder setup

Eave height 16 ft above grade. How far out should the foot be, and what working length is needed?

  1. Foot distance = 16 ft ÷ 4 = 4 ft from the wall.
  2. Length to the eave = √(16² + 4²) = √(256 + 16) = √272 = 16.49 ft.
  3. Add 3 ft extension above the landing → ≈ 19.5 ft working length.
  4. Extension ladders lose length to section overlap (ANSI: ~3 ft overlap for ladders up to 36 ft). A 24-ft extension ladder gives ≈ 21 ft working length, so it works. A 20-ft ladder gives ≈ 17 ft, which is too short.

Common wrong answers

  • "Extend 3 rungs" or "extend 36 in above the gutter" (36 in = 3 ft is correct. The trap is "3 rungs," which is only ~3 ft if rungs are 12 in apart, and is not the rule's wording).
  • "1:3 angle." No, it's 1:4.
  • "Aluminum ladder near power lines." Prohibited when contact is possible.

1.8 Power tools, pneumatic nailers, and material handling [OSHA / NIOSH]

  • Pneumatic tools: secured to the hose by a positive means to prevent disconnection (1926.302(b)(1)). Compressed air may be used for cleaning only when reduced to less than 30 psi, and only with effective chip guarding and PPE (1926.302(b)(4)).

Worked calculation: noise dose (1926.52 method)

A roofer runs a power roof cutter at 100 dBA for 1 h and works near a compressor at 95 dBA for 3 h. Permitted times: 100 dBA = 2 h, 95 dBA = 4 h. Dose = C₁/T₁ + C₂/T₂ = 1/2 + 3/4 = 0.50 + 0.75 = 1.25. That is greater than 1.0, so the exposure exceeds the PEL.

1.11 Hot work: asphalt kettles, torches, propane, and fire

Hot asphalt [NRCA-P]

  • EVT (equiviscous temperature): the temperature at which asphalt reaches the viscosity for proper application. Apply at EVT ± 25°F (mopping; mechanical spreader EVTs differ). Asphalt that is too cool gives poor adhesion and heavy moppings. Asphalt that is too hot drains off, loses properties, and increases fume and fire risk.
  • Flash point (FP): never heat asphalt to or above its flash point. The common industry rule is to keep kettle temperature at least 25°F below FP; check the bill of lading or SDS [MANUFACTURER].
  • Finished blowing temperature (FBT), listed on the bill of lading: do not hold asphalt above the FBT for extended periods.
  • Kettle safety: a trained operator stays with the kettle. Keep lids closed (close them to smother a kettle fire; never use water on an asphalt fire). Keep the kettle level, away from building openings and air intakes, with an extinguisher nearby. Use a thermometer, and keep the kettle away from combustibles. [JURISDICTION] Fire codes (e.g., IFC §303) add separation distances and extinguisher ratings, and generally prohibit kettles on roofs.
  • Burn first aid [NRCA-P]: cool with cool (not ice-cold) water. Do not try to peel asphalt off the skin or use solvents on it. Get medical care.

Torch-applied roofing: NRCA CERTA program (public NRCA document)

  • Fire watch: minimum 2 hours after the last torch is turned off, by a trained, dedicated watcher.
  • Never torch directly onto a combustible deck, or at or into concealed spaces, flutes, gaps, or wood nailers where heat can enter. Use a torch-safe method (e.g., a self-adhered or mechanically fastened base, or heat-welding tools) at flashings and penetrations [NRCA-P / CERTA].
  • Keep multiple 4A:60B:C (or similar) extinguishers close to torching. Check the current CERTA manual for exact counts and distances [EDITION].
  • Propane cylinders: upright, secured, and away from torching and heat. Shut off cylinder valves when not in use. Check hoses and regulators for leaks with soapy water, never a flame.

2.1 Roof decks: types, what "acceptable" means, and inspection

Plain English

The deck is the structural surface the roof system goes on. The roofer does not design the deck. The roofer does have to confirm it is suitable to receive roofing before starting, and report problems in writing.

What the roofer sees / decision tested

  • Before starting: is the deck dry, clean, smooth, securely fastened, free of ridges, depressions, and debris, and adequately sloped to drains?
  • If defects are found: document them and notify the owner or general contractor in writing before proceeding. Many contracts treat starting work as acceptance of the substrate. This is INFERENCE from standard specification language; check your contract. The roofer does not decide structural adequacy, but should not roof over visibly deteriorated, wet, or overstressed decking.
  • Deflection and ponding: a deck that sags between supports will pond water. A new membrane doesn't fix that. The fix is structural (framing/deck) or tapered insulation/crickets, not heavier roofing.

Common wrong answers

  • "The roofer is responsible for the structural design of the deck." The designer and the owner are.
  • "Mop the base sheet solid to LWIC." LWIC base sheets are mechanically fastened, because trapped moisture must escape.
  • "Any insulation can go over a steel deck." It must span the flute opening without crushing; thin or soft boards fail under foot traffic.

2.2 Tear-off vs. re-cover, and moisture diagnosis before reroofing

Code limits on re-cover (IRC R908 / IBC 1511, 2018–2021 model codes) [JURISDICTION]

A new covering may not be installed over an existing one when:

  1. the existing roof or covering is water-soaked or has deteriorated so it can't serve as a base;
  2. the existing covering is slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile;
  3. there are already two or more applications of any type of covering.

There are narrow exceptions (e.g., certain metal systems, or a new separate structural system). Local amendments vary.

NRCA's position [NRCA-P]: complete tear-off is generally preferred. It lets you inspect the deck and remove wet materials. Re-cover is an owner/designer decision based on a condition survey.

Moisture surveys (low-slope)

Method How it works Notes
Infrared thermography Wet insulation holds heat. After a sunny day, wet areas show warmer at dusk/night Needs a dry surface, low wind, and a good solar load. Ballast and reflective surfaces limit it
Nuclear moisture gauge Counts hydrogen (water) in the assembly with a neutron source; readings on a grid Licensed operator. Hydrogen in some insulations (e.g., foam, asphalt) raises baseline readings

Decision tested: the best way to confirm wet insulation found by an infrared scan is a core cut at the suspect area. Rescanning or "wait for the next rain" are weaker answers.

Worked calculation: point load from a pallet

A pallet holds 42 bundles of laminated shingles at ~75 lb each [MANUFACTURER: weights vary 60–90 lb]. The pallet footprint is 4 ft × 4 ft.

  1. Total load = 42 × 75 lb = 3,150 lb
  2. Area = 4 ft × 4 ft = 16 sq ft
  3. Pressure = 3,150 ÷ 16 = ≈ 197 psf

A typical residential roof design live load is 20 psf, and snow load may be 20–30 psf in Illinois [JURISDICTION]. A load of ~200 psf is roughly 10× that design load. That's why bundles are distributed across the roof and placed near bearing points. It isn't proof of failure (structures carry safety factors), but it is the reasoning the question is testing.

Type Softening point (°F) Typical use

Rule of logic: higher slope and hotter climate → higher softening point, to prevent slippage (plies sliding downslope). Exact slope limits per type depend on the reference edition and the manufacturer. Learn the ordering above for certain; treat slope cut-offs as [EDITION/MANUFACTURER].

Coal tar (ASTM D450 Type I, Type III)

  • Excellent on dead-level roofs (cold-flows and self-heals; very water-resistant).
  • Incompatible with asphalt. Don't mix coal tar and asphalt materials without the manufacturer's approved separation.
  • Coal-tar fumes are more irritating and hazardous. Minimum-slope rules differ (IBC: coal-tar BUR min. ⅛:12; other membranes ¼:12) [JURISDICTION].

Related asphalt products

  • ASTM D41 asphalt primer: applied to concrete and metal surfaces before hot asphalt or adhesives, to improve adhesion.
  • Roof cement / flashing cement (ASTM D4586, asbestos-free): trowel-grade, used for repairs and flashing. Not a substitute for proper metal/membrane flashings.
  • Cutbacks (solvent-thinned) vs. emulsions (water-based). Emulsions must not freeze before curing.

Hot-asphalt terms (see §1 for safety)

  • EVT: apply at EVT ± 25°F.
  • Flash point: never reach it.
  • FBT (finished blowing temperature): don't hold above FBT for long.

Heating asphalt too hot or too long lowers its softening point (it degrades the asphalt), which raises slippage risk. That is a classic "most likely cause" item.

Plain English

Insulation saves energy. It also gives the membrane a smooth, firm base and can create slope (tapered systems). Different boards have different R-values, compatibility, and moisture behavior.

Installation practice [NRCA-P]

  • Two or more layers with joints staggered/offset between layers. This reduces thermal bridging and air paths through joints.
  • Don't install more insulation than can be covered with membrane the same day. At day's end, install temporary water cut-offs / night seals and remove them before resuming, so they don't become buried dams.
  • Attachment: mechanically fastened (screws and plates, with the pattern set by wind zone), adhered (hot asphalt for compatible boards, or low-rise foam adhesive in beads), or loose-laid under ballast.
  • Cover board over foam insulation is recommended under adhered membranes and where traffic, hail, or fire rating matters.

Worked calculation: total R-value

An assembly has two layers of 2.0-in polyiso at R-5.7/in, plus a ½-in gypsum cover board at R-0.5. Is it R-25?

  1. Polyiso: 2 layers × 2.0 in × 5.7 = 22.8
  2. Cover board: 0.5
  3. Total ≈ 23.3. That is short of R-25 by 1.7.
  4. Extra polyiso needed = 1.7 ÷ 5.7 = 0.30 in. In practice you'd go to a thicker layer (e.g., 2.0 in + 2.5 in = 4.5 in → 25.65 + 0.5 = 26.2).

(Energy-code R-values for commercial roofs come from the Illinois Energy Conservation Code / IECC [JURISDICTION/EDITION].)

Worked calculation: tapered insulation

A drain sits at the low point, and the high point is 36 ft away. The slope is ¼ in per ft.

  1. Rise = 36 ft × 0.25 in/ft = 9 in
  2. With a 1-in minimum starter thickness at the drain, the high-side thickness = 1 + 9 = 10 in of taper, plus any flat base layers.
  3. At ⅛ in/ft the rise would be only 4.5 in. But ¼:12 is the common minimum for positive drainage (IBC 1507 for membranes) [JURISDICTION], and NRCA recommends designing for ¼:12.

2.7 Vapor retarders, air barriers, and condensation basics

Definitions (model-code classes) [JURISDICTION]

Class Permeance Examples
Class I ≤ 0.1 perm Polyethylene sheet, foil facers, some modified-bitumen vapor retarders
Class II > 0.1 to ≤ 1.0 perm Kraft facing, some self-adhered membranes
Class III > 1.0 to ≤ 10 perm Latex paint

Rules of thumb

  • Location: on the warm-in-winter side of the insulation. In an Illinois heated building, that is below the insulation, on the deck side.
  • When one is needed on a low-slope roof [NRCA-P, widely cited]: consider a vapor retarder when the outside mean January temperature is below 40°F and interior winter RH is 45% or greater. Always evaluate high-humidity occupancies (pools, laundries, paper mills, food processing, wet manufacturing). All of Illinois has January means below 40°F. [EDITION]: recent NRCA guidance leans on a dew-point/hygrothermal analysis by the designer.
  • Air leakage moves far more moisture than vapor diffusion. Unsealed deck penetrations and perimeter gaps cause condensation. The 2026 NRCA volume title itself, "…Condensation and Air Leakage Control," reflects that emphasis.
  • Over steel decks: install a substrate board (e.g., gypsum) on the flutes first, then the vapor retarder, then insulation.
  • Dew point: the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water condenses. If any layer in the assembly is colder than the indoor air's dew point, and moist air can reach it, condensation forms there.

Diagnosis pattern: drips or ceiling stains in cold, dry weather that stop when it warms up, with no rain → condensation/air leakage, not a membrane leak.

2.10 Estimating quantities: the core math (Atcheson-style; methods are standard)

Key units

  • 1 square = 100 sq ft of roof surface.
  • Slope = rise per 12 in of run (e.g., 6:12). Pitch = rise ÷ span (6:12 slope = ¼ pitch). Tests mix the two; don't.

Slope (pitch) factors: roof area = plan area × factor

Factor = √(rise² + 12²) ÷ 12

Slope 3:12 4:12 5:12 6:12 7:12 8:12 9:12 10:12 12:12
Factor 1.031 1.054 1.083 1.118 1.158 1.202 1.250 1.302 1.414

Hip/valley length factor (per foot of common-rafter run, for equal slopes) = √(rise² + 288) ÷ 12. For 6:12: √(36 + 288) = √324 = 18 → 1.5.

Worked calculation A: squares and bundles

A gable roof has a plan area (including overhangs) of 40 ft × 30 ft, a slope of 6:12, and a 10% waste allowance. The shingles run 3 bundles per square.

  1. Plan area = 40 × 30 = 1,200 sq ft
  2. Roof area = 1,200 × 1.118 = 1,341.6 sq ft = 13.42 squares
  3. Add 10% waste: 13.42 × 1.10 = 14.76 squares → order 15 squares
  4. Bundles = 15 × 3 = 45 bundles

(Waste allowances vary by roof complexity. Roughly 10% for simple gables and 15%+ for cut-up hip roofs is a common industry rule of thumb, not a code figure.)

Worked calculation B: shingles and nails per square

  • 3-tab 12 in × 36 in at 5-in exposure: exposed area per shingle = 5 × 36 = 180 sq in. Shingles per square = 14,400 ÷ 180 = 80. At 4 nails each = 320 nails/sq; at 6 nails (high-wind) = 480 nails/sq.
  • Metric laminate 13¼ in × 39⅜ in at 5⅝-in exposure: 5.625 × 39.375 = 221.5 sq in → 14,400 ÷ 221.5 = ≈ 65 shingles/sq.

Worked calculation C: ridge cap

A 40-ft ridge with 12-in cap pieces at 5-in exposure: 40 ft × 12 in/ft ÷ 5 in = 96 pieces. With 3 pieces cut per 3-tab shingle → 32 shingles. (Laminated ridge products list their own linear-foot coverage per bundle [MANUFACTURER].)

Worked calculation D: drip edge

Same roof as calculation A (the 40 × 30 ft plan already includes overhangs, so each half-span is 15 ft horizontal).

  1. Eaves = 2 × 40 ft = 80 ft
  2. Rakes: 15 ft × 1.118 = 16.77 ft each, × 4 rake edges = 67.1 ft
  3. Total = 147.1 LF
  4. 10-ft pieces lapped 2 in give a net 9.83 ft each → 147.1 ÷ 9.83 = 14.96 → 15 pieces (most estimators would order 16 to allow for cutting waste).

Worked calculation E: ice barrier width (code version) [JURISDICTION]

The IRC requires the ice barrier to extend from the eave to at least 24 in inside the exterior wall line (R905.1.2). Overhang = 12 in horizontal; slope = 6:12.

  1. Horizontal distance = 12 in (overhang) + 24 in = 36 in
  2. Along the slope: 36 × 1.118 = 40.2 in
  3. One 36-in-wide course is not enough. You need a second course (lapped per the manufacturer).

Some manufacturers and designers measure from the interior face of the wall. That adds the wall thickness and is more conservative. Read the stem.

Worked calculation F: underlayment rolls

ASTM D226 Type I ("No. 15") rolls nominally cover 4 squares. Type II ("No. 30") rolls cover about 2 squares. Synthetic rolls vary (commonly 10 squares) [MANUFACTURER].

  • 15 squares of No. 30 → 15 ÷ 2 = 7.5 → 8 rolls. For a low-slope double layer (see §3), double it → 15 rolls.

Worked calculation G: BUR materials (NRCA-style rates) [EDITION/MANUFACTURER]

A 4-ply glass-fiber BUR on insulation, 100 squares, with a gravel surface.

  • Interply mopping: ≈ 25 lb asphalt per square per mopping (NRCA commonly cites 20–25 lb ± 15%). With 4 moppings (base + 3 interply): 4 × 25 × 100 = 10,000 lb
  • Flood coat: ≈ 60 lb/sq (asphalt) → 6,000 lb
  • Aggregate: ≈ 400 lb/sq gravel (≈ 300 lb/sq slag) → 40,000 lb = 20 tons
  • Total asphalt ≈ 16,000 lb (8 tons)
